# Startup Idle

A satirical incremental/idle game about scaling a tech startup by any means necessary — hire interns, unleash vibe coders, anoint 10x devs, and watch an isometric city grow as your headcount does. Built as a single-page React app with a PixiJS-rendered city.

## Gameplay

You found a startup as one of several **founder archetypes**, each bending a different progression curve, then grow the company through interlocking systems:

- **Employees (generators)** — three roles (`intern`, `vibe_coder`, `10x_dev`) that produce money and innovation each tick. Hire more and buy **upgrades** to scale output.
- **Innovation** — a soft global multiplier (`1 + logMult · log₁₀(innovation+1)`) on all output, plus the currency for **managers** and unlocks.
- **Managers** — auto-tiering multipliers for money / innovation / valuation; assign them and they progress on their own.
- **Valuation & board mandates** — valuation accrues from revenue; spend it on permanent global mandates.
- **Employee management & satisfaction** — perks (money/innovation/cost/auto-buy) and a morale system that drifts each tick and feeds back into output.
- **Team leaders** — one named employee per role (`intern`, `vibe_coder`, `10x_dev`). Hire from a pool of 3 candidates, each with a random **trait** (e.g. `overachiever`, `chaos_agent`, `brown_noser`) that sets their output/morale bias and unlocks a 4-skill pool. Employees accumulate `outputStat` and `moraleStat` over time, gaining **ranks** that award skill points to spend on up to 3 skills (max level 3 each) — revenue multipliers, IPS boosts, satisfaction offsets, rank-compounding effects, and more. Skills have cross-role effects: `chaos_spark` debuffs *other* roles' satisfaction; `team_morale` buffs *all* roles. Certain trait pairings unlock **pair synergies** (e.g. `productive_chaos`, `dumpster_fire`) with additional output-growth or satisfaction penalties. Firing an employee costs innovation per rank and leaves a permanent **legacy bonus** (rev + IPS) for that role.
- **Vape shop** — 53 achievements across all game systems earn *vape juice*; spend it on 12 impactful upgrades (money output, innovation, valuation rate, hire cost reduction, equity payout) that visibly modify a high-def SVG vape rendered as a compact overlay on the map canvas (bottom-left). Achievements and upgrades persist through prestige resets. Version `0.2.0` bumped the upgrade roster; returning players' vape progress resets on load.
- **Slop pit** — a toxic sludge pool (`state/slop-pit.store.ts`) that unlocks at 50 vibe coders and slowly fills based on headcount. Occupies a reserved 5×5 plot in the vibe_coder district (world-gen skips building placement there). Above 50% fill it applies an income penalty (up to −85% at 100%). Draining costs −40 vibe_coder satisfaction. Renders in the isometric city with a fixed 5×5 pit footprint (camera-facing walls, dark floor), sludge that rises vertically from the pit bottom to ground level as fill increases, and a real-time height-field wave simulation (`office/slop-pit-fluid.ts`) that propagates ripple rings across the 5×5 surface grid. Drips from animated pipe fittings land on the sludge and inject splash impulses into the wave field; bubble rings float at the live sludge surface level. A DRAIN button empties the pit.
- **AI singularity** — an ominous meter that creeps up when vibe coders are miserable.
- **Founders** — six archetypes (Hacker, Bootstrapper, Visionary, Hustler, Agentic Delusionist, NEET) each with a unique *per-founder scaling modifier* that grows stronger with every acquisition made as that specific founder (tracked in `state/exits.store.ts`, persisted). Dynamic perk text shows current bonus levels. NEET starts with $5 and no base bonuses but doubles all money output with every exit (×2^n). Chosen on a full-screen select before the game begins; cards show the exit count badge and a scaling-modifier description.
- **Offline progression** — on return, the real game tick is replayed in chunks over the time away (full credit, capped at 2 days), with a "while you were away" popup.
- **Company Acquisition (prestige)** — once you've built enough *total accrued valuation*, accept an acquisition offer to bank permanent **Equity** (diminishing-returns payout), soft-reset into a fresh company (re-picking a founder; Equity, skill tree and board mandates persist), and spend Equity in a Path-of-Exile-style **skill tree** (~330 procedurally-laid-out nodes across themed clusters with standalone keystones, rendered on its own PixiJS viewport — verified overlap-free, light/dark themed). Costs escalate per node allocated; hovering previews the cheapest route (with total cost) and a click allocates the whole route at once; the tree is **respeccable** (Exits buy persistent respec points; one click refunds a node and its dependents). Includes fuzzy node **search** and hover-to-spotlight from the allocated-bonuses panel. The full loop — accrual tracking, payout, soft reset, allocation, respec, persistence — is in, and node **effects are wired to the economy**: every allocated grant resolves (`resolvePrestigeModifiers`) into multipliers applied at the same chokepoints as the founder modifiers (money, innovation, valuation, employee output, headcount synergy, automation, manager speed, hire cost, singularity, satisfaction-gain rate, Equity payout). **All keystones are fully implemented**, including their structural reshapes — Bootstrapped disables managers & auto-buy, Crunch Mode switches satisfaction off, Enshittify halves positive satisfaction bonuses, AGI-Pilled cripples interns, Permanent Acqui-hire grants free starting intern levels — each surfaced in the relevant UI (see `docs/acquisition-skill-tree-design.md`).
- **Isometric city** — a PixiJS + pixi-viewport scene that visualizes each district's headcount as growing building stacks, with hover highlighting and HTML info popovers.

## Architecture notes

- **State & persistence.** Each system is a Zustand store under `src/state`, persisted to `localStorage` via the `persist` middleware. Large numbers are `break_infinity.js` `Decimal`s serialized via `decimalReplacer`/`decimalReviver` (see `state/_break_infinity.decimals.ts`); store `merge` functions use `coerceDecimal` to defensively re-hydrate any legacy/foreign shape so a malformed save can't crash on first render.
- **Version wipe.** `state/version.store.ts` holds `CURRENT_VERSION`; bumping the major or minor triggers a full progress wipe (storage + in-memory) on next load via `useCompareVersion`. Use this for breaking changes.
- **The game loop** runs in `App.tsx` (a `setInterval`), ticking generators and managers. Stores expose per-second getters (`getMoneyPerSecond`, `getInnovationPerSecond`, `getValuationPerSecond`) that mirror the tick's multiplier chains, so displayed rates match actual earnings.
- **Isometric renderer.** Buildings are derived deterministically from headcount (`office/city/compute-city.ts`) and drawn into depth-sorted Pixi containers (`cityDepthKey`, column-dominant). Heights are monotonic in count to avoid "floor stealing."
- **World v2** (`src/world`, route `?world=1`) is a separate, mutable map engine that shares only the pure iso maths in `src/iso`; v1 under `src/office` is untouched. Terrain lives in dense typed arrays indexed `y*w+x` rather than keyed sets, so an edit updates one sprite per changed cell instead of rebuilding the map. Sprites are grouped into **diagonal bands** (one container per `x+y`): within a band sprites are a full tile apart at identical Y and cannot overlap, so nothing inside a band ever needs sorting. Elevation is in **half steps** (`HEIGHT_UNIT = 16.5`) and moves `wy` only — never `wx` — which is why band order and picking both stay cheap under verticality. Undo/redo is cell-index patches (`edit/commands.ts`), one command per drag stroke.
- **Verticality.** `height` is signed and measured in HALF steps (16.5px), because the tileset has exactly two rises — ×0.5 and ×1 — and nothing between; a full step is one slab skirt, so the field stays integral. Height moves `wy` only, never `wx`, which is why elevation costs the renderer nothing. A cell taller than its visible neighbours grows a **column**: copies of its own tile stacked at `HEIGHT_STEP`, each one's top hidden by the copy above, so only the skirts show — the tileset's skirt already shades its two faces differently, so nothing is tinted by hand. Columns live in their own per-band tier beneath the tiles, the one place ordering within a band matters.
- **Picking under elevation.** A screen point is ambiguous once the ground is not flat, but only along ONE diagonal, because `wx` fixes `x − y` whatever the height. `pickCell` marches that diagonal front to back and takes the first cell whose top face contains the point; there is exactly one candidate per band, since `x − y` must be an integer within 1 of the point's own and the two options have opposite parity. `faceCoords` solves the face exactly, so a **ramp's tilted parallelogram** works the same as a flat diamond. Tested against a brute-force scan rather than against the previous implementation, whose answers at shared face edges came out of rounding.
- **The `crossing` fixture is both storeys running at once** (`buildCrossing`, in the editor's terrain-fixture row). A road east to west, a channel north to south, and a deck carrying the road over the water where they meet — fed at both, so there is always water running ALONG the road and over the span, and water running DOWN the channel and under it, and the two never meet. It is the scene every fix to this took, because each failure shows up in it plainly: water pouring off the road into the channel at the mouth of the span (no abutment), water running off the sides as waterfalls instead of standing (no parapet), the surface doubling where the road meets the deck (ghosts drawn twice), or nothing crossing at all. Small on purpose — 24² — because all of that is a few tiles across and a 64² map puts it under a thumbnail.
- **A GHOST IS NEVER DRAWN**, which is what makes the surface join. A ghost is a window onto the storey below and that storey already draws it — left standing, the road's water at the mouth of every span was painted TWICE, once by each mesh at the same level, so the surface abruptly doubled where it met the bridge and the upper mesh ended in a hard edge a tile short of it. Cleared after each step, each surface is drawn exactly once and the two meet at the same level. Measured along a lane: road 1.58, span 1.03, road beyond 2.01, with the ghosts reading nought. The catch is that the last substep's drift is still sitting in the depth when it is cleared, so it has to be booked first — and the depth has to be SEEDED alongside the level it is held at, or the next frame's first substep books the ghost as having just lost everything it holds.
- **A BRIDGE HAS AN ABUTMENT, which is the one thing a heightfield cannot say** (`ColumnField.closed`). Where a road meets a span the channel is UNDER the deck and the road does not pour into it — there is a wall. But a height per cell makes that a cliff, and water goes over cliffs, so to the storey below the road simply ended at a drop into the river: measured on a ditch with a span over it, of **160 poured on the approach, 11 reached the deck and 43 fell in the ditch**. Water was getting onto a bridge by going through it. So an edge can be closed, and exactly the edges between a deck and the road that meets it are — which is the deck's own OWNED-against-GHOST classification read a second time. The edges from a deck to the CHANNEL either side stay open, because that is the river passing under and closing it dams the bridge; both halves are tested, and each sabotage trips its own. On a walled lane where the span is the only crossing: **nothing at all in the ditch, and the water gets to the far side.**
- **It is the fourth rule the two solvers have to agree on**, so it is written twice on purpose like `spill` and held by `?gpucheck`. Adding it grew the WGSL `Consts` struct by a `vec4` and `CONSTS_BYTES` did not follow, which is a device rejecting every pass with "bound with size 320, the pipeline requires 336" — and the thing worth keeping is how that hid: the behaviour I was measuring came from HOST code, so water still climbed onto the bridge and the map still looked right while the device was failing every frame. The compare harness is what caught it.
- **A BRIDGE IS A PIECE OF ROAD, and the margin round a deck is two different things** (`STOREY` in `fluid/columns.ts`). The first version modelled every non-deck cell as a HOLE, and that one decision made the feature useless: a deck could only be poured on directly and could only lose water by having it deleted, so water would not run onto a bridge from the road that meets it, nor off the far end onto the road beyond. Off the SIDE of a span the ground has fallen away and a hole is right. At either END the land is at the deck's own level and the surface is continuous — those columns are GHOSTS, a window onto the storey below, held at what that storey has. The flow between a deck and the road it meets is then worked out by the solver across an ordinary edge, and how far a ghost drifted from what it was held at is exactly what crossed: no flux arithmetic, and no second account of how water moves. It settles up through `addWater` with a SIGNED amount, which clamps at nothing and records the real delta into the arrivals list — the only way the host may change water the DEVICE owns, and writing a depth directly settled correctly on the host and silently not at all on the device.
- **And the upper storey is stepped on BOTH paths** (`stepUpper`). Folded into `stepWater` it was skipped entirely whenever the GPU solver was built, which is the default — so nothing ever flowed on or off a bridge on the path the game actually runs, which looked exactly like the coupling being broken when the coupling had never once executed.
- **WATER STANDS ON A BRIDGE, on a second storey** (`WaterField.over`, `setStorey` in `fluid/columns.ts`). The picker resolves a click to the surface you can SEE, and a span is that surface where there is one — but every tool wrote the terrain, so a pour aimed at a bridge landed on the riverbed **twenty half steps below it**, out of sight under its own deck. The fix is a second field over the same map: ground at the deck where there is one, and NO FLOOR anywhere else, so water running off the end of a span falls out of that field and is handed to the one below. Volume is conserved across the handover, asserted on a closed pan every step. It is the SAME solver run twice rather than a second, cheaper set of rules for how water behaves — a deck is flat and a puddle on one has little to do, so a bespoke thing was tempting, and a bespoke thing is exactly the duplicated rule every seam in this water has come from. Nothing at all is allocated on a map with no deck on it.
- **`Grid.pool` still goes on the GROUND**, which is the bug that came with it: pool is a LAND layer — the river in its channel, the lake in its basin — and routing it through the ordinary pour meant a map that opened with a river under a bridge put that stretch of it on TOP of the bridge. It writes to the lower storey directly now.
- **A deck's sides are a PARAPET** (`DECK_KERB`), and getting that number's SIGN wrong broke the feature twice over. It started as the land far below, which drew a translucent pane sixteen half steps tall along every edge of every span. Then it became a slab BELOW the deck, which is worse than it sounds: the columns off the side are emptied every substep, so a drop there is a permanent downhill into a bottomless drain — water poured on a bridge ran off both edges inside half a second and could never accumulate, and a drop at a lip is exactly what the solver spawns FALLS from, so both sides of every span ran as waterfalls the whole time anything was on them. A bridge has parapets. A slab ABOVE the deck holds a puddle where it is put, lets a flood go over the side and fall, and settles the rendering question too, because ground higher than the water has no face to draw. Measured on a generated map: **96 units poured on a span, 77 still there a second later and steady**, against draining to nothing before.
- **And it is DRAWN there, by a second mesh.** Both water layers used to take the world's `WaterField` and reach into its `columns`, which meant exactly one storey could ever be drawn — so a bridge's water was simulated, conserved and invisible. They take a `ColumnField` now, which is all either ever wanted, and the scene builds a second layer over `field.over` into the SAME bands, added after the one below: a span is nearer the camera than the bed it crosses, and `bandOf` is `x + y` for both. The upper storey is stepped on the HOST whatever is running the one below — what stands on a bridge is a puddle, and handing a second field to the device would double the state it owns to carry a few hundred columns of water.
- **THE CLEARANCE RULE IS GONE, and a river crosses the street** (`free` in `world/gen/valley.ts`, `carveChannel`). A channel used to keep two tiles clear of any paving, and that single condition shaped every map this generator has made: the road spans the map, so the cells a river could use fell into two regions with no way between them, every course had to begin and end on ONE side of the street, and river and road ran roughly parallel on every seed because it was the only shape left. With a deck there is a third answer — where the cut goes under paving, the paving is carried over it at the road's own level. **16 of 16 seeds now have a crossing**, the road is **one network on every one of them**, and frontage is untouched at 128 against a minimum of 12. Lakes still keep their distance: a lake has no span over it and nothing to gain from drowning somebody's frontage.
- **And a crossing gets HEADROOM**, because the cone pins the land at a paved cell to the road's own height — so the valley floor there *is* the road's height, and the floor is the line the channel fills to. The first version put the water exactly at the underside of the span. The floor is now held two slabs below the road wherever the course comes within a valley's width of it, taken as a running minimum like every other constraint on the bed so the river keeps descending past the crossing instead of stepping back up after it.
- **The bug that came with it is worth keeping.** Ramps are DERIVED from paving and height, and every surface edit re-derives the ones around it — including pouring water, which is a surface tool. The derivation's height reader still read the terrain, and the terrain under a span is the riverbed, which steps a slab at a time exactly as a road running downhill does. So it cut ramps across the bridge, and what that looked like was **slopes appearing in the road the moment you poured anywhere near it**. The road MASK had been taught to read the surface; the ramp derivation had not. Measured across eight generated maps: **149 ramps cut across the street from the terrain, none from the surface.** Both halves are now tests, including the wrong one, so the right one cannot pass vacuously.
- **The cell readout is a toggle now** (`Overlays.readout`, `?readout=0`). It follows the cursor, which means it sits on top of the tiles you are hovering in order to look at them — so it can be turned off, from the overlays row like every other switch for looking, or from the URL so a reload keeps it off. Written as a VALUE rather than a bare presence flag like `?gpucheck` because unlike those it is ON by default, and a flag whose presence turns something on cannot express turning it off. A value it does not recognise leaves it on: the failure modes are not symmetric, since a readout that will not go away is a nuisance you can see, and one that silently never appears looks like the editor being broken.
- **A DECK is the one place the world stops being a heightfield** (`Grid.deck`, `Grid.deckZ`). Every other layer gives a cell exactly one surface, and that is not a detail: `bandOf` is `x + y` with height deliberately absent, because for a heightfield a cell with a larger `x + y` is physically nearer whatever either height is — so painter's order is free, and the picker's front-to-back march down one diagonal is correct for the same reason. A deck is a SECOND surface over a cell with a gap under it, which is what a bridge is. The band order survives it: two surfaces in one cell share a band, and the upper one draws after the lower, which is right. The picker prefers the deck, and that is right too — a top face is the whole diamond, so nothing of the ground under a deck is visible *through* it; what you see under a bridge is the cells either side.
- **And the gap is real because the deck is never told to the water** (`syncGround`). A deck is not terrain: the ground beneath it is untouched, so the solver knows nothing about the bridge and the river runs under it without anything having to say so. Lifting it into the column grounds the way a building's footprint is lifted would dam the channel it spans, which is the opposite of a bridge — a test asserts the river is **identical, tile for tile and volume for volume, with a span over it and without one**, and that sabotage is exactly what trips it. It is stated as an identity rather than a tolerance because two DRY maps are also identical, so the same test checks there is water under the span.
- **A deck is paved ground at a level of its own**, which is the whole of its integration with the road system. The material lives in `paved` — saying what it is made of twice is two things to keep in step — and `deck` is a one-byte flag meaning "this paved surface is in the air". Everything else falls out of `mask.ts` asking for the SURFACE height instead of the terrain: ask for the terrain and a span over a river compares riverbed against riverbed, which is uneven by design since the channel is cut with a bed that falls, and every tile of the bridge is a dead end. Measured on the `bridge` fixture: 24 deck tiles over ground ranging −3 to 3, laid level at 13, resolving to **one road network**. `ramp-derive` needed nothing — it already read height through an injected function.
- **Ramps are DERIVED from paving and height**, not placed by hand. A road crossing a step needs a ramp to be traversable, so the ramp is what makes the road a road rather than a separate decision: pave between two heights and it appears, erase the road or level the ground and it goes. The ramp always sits on the LOWER cell rising toward its higher neighbour, so a staircase ramps continuously. `roads/ramp-derive.ts` refuses what the artset cannot draw — a half step (no paved ×0.5 art), a step of two or more, or a cell facing two higher neighbours — and the road simply stays split, which the network overlay shows. Ramp frames differ in height by direction: N and E rise up-screen and are 131px tall, while S and W rise toward the viewer and fit the raise inside the 99px a flat tile already has, which is why a slope sprite is anchored by the standard ground height rather than its own frame. The whole derivation happens inside the paving command (via `PatchBuilder.peek`, which reads the staged edit rather than the grid), so one undo reverses road and ramp together. `ramp-art.ts` reads the hand-authored `slope-labels.json` and filters it to walkable ground: no water (most gently tilted art is river and shoreline), no road-extent embankments, no corner rises.
- **Roads are a paved AREA, not a line.** Forced by the artwork: the tileset has no thin outer corner, so a line model cannot draw a bend. You paint cells and the region autotiles, which also gets 2-wide avenues and plazas for free rather than special-casing them. The `mask → sprite` table is **derived** from `road-labels.json` / `city-tile-labels.json` at load, not hand-written — v1's hand-written table omits all four outer corners even though the art is labelled, so every v1 bend falls back to plain asphalt. `roadCoverage()` walks all 16 masks and reports which resolve exactly, which substituted and which have no art, because a silent fallback is what hides the failure — and the editor's `gaps` overlay marks the substituted cells on the map, so a labelling gap is visible rather than deduced. A turn picks its art from the DIAGONAL across its two open sides: where the paved region wraps round, the cell is a corner of a block and the road fills it (square-edged art); where it does not, a single-width road is turning, and the artset draws that only as a CURVE. Both are labelled, so the landscape set resolves every mask exactly; `roadCoverage` fails loudly if that ever regresses.
- **A BRIDGE IS A ROAD, BUT NOT AN INTERCHANGEABLE ONE** (`dev/roadlabel.html`, `roads/table.ts`). The labeller has a `bridge` tick and a set of *water out* edges beside the usual role and open edges, because a span IS an ordinary straight — same role, same connections, resolved by the same rules — and giving it its own vocabulary would mean the engine and the tool disagreed about the same tile. What the tick buys is a **partition**: `buildRoadTable(set, deck)` indexes the two families separately, and the reason is asymmetric. A span drawn on dry land paints a river across a field; a plain straight drawn over the channel runs the road into the water. Both are wrong, but only the first happens by ACCIDENT — a bridge frame left in `byOpen` is in the fallback pool for every straight on the map, so the first tile anybody labels starts turning up in the middle of fields. The test for it runs against a label record handed in rather than the real file, because until somebody labels a bridge the real file cannot exercise it, and by the time it can the damage is done. The *water out* edges default to the two the road does not use and are drawn DOTTED until confirmed, so an unchecked guess never looks like an answer — the artset has spans that meet a bank, where the water leaves by one edge only, and nothing but a person can see that.
- **Derived road art is baked, not composited at runtime.** Where three paved cells meet a grass cell at a vertex the kerb has to turn 90° around that point, and no single tile carries a mixture of interior and notched corners. `bun run bake:road-corners` composites the 22 needed variants into `isometric_assets/derived/roadCorners_sheet.png`, which loads as a fourth atlas — so at runtime they are ordinary frames with the same nearest-neighbour sampling and the same batching as every other tile. Compositing at draw time was tried twice and fails twice over: separate sprites round to the pixel grid independently (a 1px drift at arbitrary zoom), and a `RenderTexture` defaults to linear filtering while the atlas is nearest (visibly soft). The variant list is derived from the autotile resolver, so the sheet cannot drift from what the engine asks for.
- **Dirty cells accumulate, and the renderer drains them.** Several commits inside one task collapse into a single React re-render, so a dirty set that each commit replaced left the renderer reconciling only the last one — every earlier edit in the grid and absent from the screen. It lives outside the store alongside `history` and the road graph, for the same in-place-mutation reason.
- **Structures are a definition plus a render STRATEGY** (`src/world/structures`). A `StructureDef` is data — a footprint, a `RenderSpec`, and rules for where it may stand — and nothing in `def.ts` or `place.ts` imports Pixi, so the placement rules are testable without a renderer. Drawing goes through a registry keyed on `render.kind` (`tiles`, `excavation`, `custom`), each strategy implementing `mount`/`update`/`unmount` against the band containers; an unregistered strategy resolves to null so one undrawable building never stops the world drawing. This is the abstraction v1 lacks: the slop pit IS a structure, but its footprint lives as hardcoded span constants inside `GroundRoadLayer`, so nothing can ask what occupies a cell or place a second one. `structureAt` stores a structure's **id**, never a list index — an index is not stable under demolish, nor under undo restoring records out of order. Placing is ONE command: level the footprint to its lower median, clear any ramp under it, stamp the footprint, create the record — so a single undo puts the hill back. `clearsTerrain` is a RENDER rule and not a data one; zeroing the terrain layer would lose the material, so demolishing could not restore it and every excavation would leave a permanent hole. The map file carries records as readable JSON and rebuilds `structureAt` by stamping them, which keeps 16KB per 64² map out of the file and means the layer cannot disagree with the records it indexes. Building stacks come from `composeBuilding` in `src/iso/kits.ts`, which v1 calls too — the rules for which frame sits on which floor are art facts and must not fork.
- **Water is a field of COLUMNS, simulated** (`src/fluid/columns.ts`, wired up in `src/world/water`, drawn by `render/water.ts`). Every cell holds a depth above whatever the ground is there, and the state is those depths plus the flux on the edges between them. A cell's surface is `ground + depth`, so water at any height flows to any other height — the only thing driving it is the difference between two surfaces, and **a waterfall is not a special case, it is a large head difference across one edge**. The scheme is virtual pipes (Mei, Decaudin & Neyret 2007): flux accelerated by the head TIMES the water able to carry it, slowed by drag, depths from the divergence. **Both sides of an edge are measured from the SILL between them**, not from the sea floor — hydrostatic reconstruction, after Audusse et al. 2004 — without which the scheme is only honest over gentle ground. Water standing on a plateau with water ten steps below it was driven by a head of ten, a pressure gradient no depth of water in the cell could produce: it evacuated the cell in a tenth of a second and left a mound where a sheet should have been, worse the higher the plateau, because the head WAS the cliff. Measured from the sill, that same edge is driven by the half step actually standing above it, and a sheet drains off a four step step and a thirty step cliff at exactly the same rate. That depth factor is what makes the BED shape the flow rather than only steer it — an edge carries in proportion to the water standing above the higher of its two grounds, so a ridge with an inch over it throttles the lake behind it instead of draining it at the lake's full weight, and a wave runs at `sqrt(gravity * depth)`: quickly across a deep pool, crawling over a shoal. The consequence you notice is channelling — cut a gully down a hillside and a sheet released across the top finds it, because the water that reaches it is deeper and deeper water pulls harder. In the editor, two gullies down a hill hold 70% of everything that gets past its foot, in four rows out of sixty-four. **The bed has its own drag**, Chézy-shaped — quadratic in the flow and falling off with the depth, applied implicitly so a quadratic cannot overshoot through zero — so a river barely notices the ground it runs over while a sheet a fraction as deep is stopped by it: fed down the same slope, a 3-deep channel runs at 2.4 tiles a second against a 0.4-deep film's 1.2, and with no bed at all the film is if anything the faster of the two. It is the GROUND's drag, one value for the map; `drag` is the FLUID's, per material, and the two are not interchangeable — a flat per-second retention takes the same fraction off a torrent and a trickle. It is also what stops a flood emptying a basin past the sill that should hold it: momentum alone overshoots, and what crosses a ridge cannot come back. Because the bed now does the dissipating, the FLUID's own damping is weak — it was carrying all of it, and a disturbed basin lost 99% of its motion in twenty seconds, which is water behaving like jelly; at 0.9 a quarter of a disturbance survives a minute and that minute is spent ringing in the basin's own standing waves, whose periods come from its shape for free.
- **A breaking wave LOSES energy, which is the sink the scheme was missing.** Foam (`render/foam.ts`) only ever drew breaking: it reads the flow, paints it white and tells the water nothing. Real breaking is not a colour — it is where a wave's organised motion is turned into turbulence and lost, and in deep water it is the dominant sink there is, the thing that stops a sea growing without bound under its own wind. Without it the only sinks here are a flat per-second drag on the flux and a bed friction that falls off as one over the depth SQUARED, so deep water has almost nothing taking energy out of it at all — and what it does have is blind to scale, taking the same fraction per second off a ten tile swell as off a one column spike. So water poured in stood up in spikes twelve half steps tall and shed them only slowly after you stopped. The model is an eddy viscosity (Kennedy, Chen, Kirby & Dalrymple 2000, after Zelt 1991): a column is breaking when its surface moves faster than a fraction of the speed the wave is travelling at, and while it does, its momentum is diffused into the columns around it at `delta^2 * h * d(eta)/dt` — a mixing length squared over a time, where the length is the depth. It is scale SELECTIVE in the way the drags are not, because a one column spike has an enormous second derivative and a ten tile swell has almost none. **Two thresholds, not one**: breaking starts hard at 0.65 of the wave speed and stops soft at 0.15, sliding between them over five depths travelled, because on a single threshold a wave flickers in and out several times a second and a dissipation that switches at the grid's own rate is exactly the thing a scheme rings on. And **implicit**, by two Jacobi sweeps, which is the whole difference between this working and not. The explicit form has a stability limit of a diffusion number of an eighth and the modelled viscosity runs a couple of hundred times past it, so clamped, every breaking column got the SAME maximum smoothing whether it was barely breaking or coming apart — the model's nought-to-one ramp meant nothing, and scaling the coefficient by a hundred changed the answer by a third, which is the signature of a term that is saturated rather than graded. What it damaged it damaged at every setting: a drain lost a third of its throughput and rivers stopped running. Solved implicitly the answer is BOUNDED however large the viscosity — even one sweep is an average of numbers already present, so it can smooth but never overshoot — and the drain draws its full rate again. Two other things had to be right. It diffuses the VELOCITY and not the discharge, because the published term diffuses `h u` and divides by `h`, and diffusing the discharge alone moves momentum between columns of very different depth as though they were the same water, which over rolling ground is most of the pairs there are. And it exchanges only between WET neighbours: read a dry cell as a velocity of zero and every waterline becomes a wall for the turbulence to drag the flow down against, and a river is nearly all bank. Measured, it is exactly as selective as it should be — a gentle wave is untouched to within 2%, a wind-blown pond settles at the identical chop of 0.164, and a wave big enough to break loses 40% of its crest in the first second. Poured on for ten seconds, the surface roughness goes from twelve half steps to two. It costs 0.5ms a frame on a settled flooded 64² map and 0.75ms while something is actually pouring.
- **Nothing else puts energy IN, so the water is never quite still.** Every other term is a sink, and a solver made only of sinks answers "what does water do when you leave it alone" with "nothing, forever". A wind stress does: a couple of long gusts drifting across the map at periods that do not divide into one another, so the balance the water is chasing keeps moving and it never arrives — a steady wind would tilt a pond once and go quiet again. It pushes the FLUX, so not a drop is created or lost, and the motion is whatever each basin makes of the forcing rather than a look imposed on it. Gated by depth, which is a deliberate lie: a stress on the surface does not care what is under it, but ungated a gust strong enough to stir a lake threw a puddle around in waves as deep as the puddle, and the things that really hold a puddle still — friction and having no fetch to raise a wave over — are not things half-tile cells can represent. Momentum on the edges is what makes a river run rather than ooze. **The step is sized by the deepest water present, because a wave runs at `sqrt(gravity * depth)`** and the deepest water is the fastest thing in the field. Once a wave outruns a cell in a step the scheme rings instead of settling, and it rings in a way that looks exactly like a damping failure and is not one — it survives the wind being turned off, the bed drag raised eightfold and the fluid drag doubled, and it goes away entirely at half the substep. **Lower the ground under a pool and you reach it in four clicks**, because lowering deepens the water by however far you lowered: a pool that settled at 24 half steps deep stood up in spikes thirty half steps tall at 45, and the ringing pumped volume as well as energy, digging its own pit to 112 half steps where the lowering had made 58. So the substep is `Courant * cell / sqrt(gravity * depth)` rather than a flat sixtieth, and the deepest column is tracked by the divergence, which writes every depth anyway. The Courant number is 0.4, which is not the one the theory gives you: the condition is `c dt / dx <= 1` in one dimension and `1/sqrt(2)` on a square grid, and on FLAT ground that second number is about right — a pond at 0.69 settles and one at 0.80 never does. Over rolling ground it is nowhere near, because the bed here is a STAIRCASE (height belongs to a tile and there are four columns to a tile, so every tile boundary is a step under the water) and every step reflects a little of every wave that crosses it. Measured on one scene poured to different depths, 0.46 settles to a millimetre of chop and 0.49 stands up in spikes and stays there; the same relief smoothed to column resolution is stable throughout, which is what says it is the steps and not the slope. Stepping finer costs time rather than truth, and only as the square root of the depth — a pond four times deeper needs twice the substeps, so a flooded 64² map costs 2.2ms at six half steps deep, 5ms at twenty and 6.4ms at a hundred and twenty. `hMax` survives as a BACKSTOP for when a frame runs out of substeps, at the theoretical limit rather than the working one, and computed from the frame's cap and not the substep so that it is UNIFORM: a cap that lands on the water's own depth is worse than one miles above it, because then some edges are capped and their neighbours are not and the switching is itself a thing a scheme can ring on. Measured, a 96 deep pond with the cap at 96 came apart after six seconds while the same pond with the cap at 50 — every edge capped, all of them consistently — sat still. Wet/dry fronts — where these schemes usually break — are handled by scaling each cell's outflows down to the water it actually has *before* anything is applied, so depths are non-negative by construction; one pass suffices, because reducing an outflow can only reduce a neighbour's inflow. A minimum SLOPE stands in for friction, which is what gives a puddle an edge instead of a film that spreads forever; a minimum DEPTH was tried first and makes water cling halfway down a cliff, since a hillside always has a gradient but not always depth.
- **The solver works in TILES, so resolution is a detail setting.** Each column knows how wide it is (`cell`, in tiles) and every length is divided by it: a head is the surface difference across one cell but what accelerates water is the GRADIENT, a flux crosses one edge but the depth it changes is spread over one cell's area, and the friction threshold is a slope rather than a head. Without this, `COLUMNS_PER_TILE` was a physics setting in disguise — halving the cell halved the head across it for the same hillside, so a finer grid was a slower, stickier fluid, and the same pour off the same plateau settled over 986 tiles at two columns and 385 at four. It now settles over the same ground at one, two and four, and gets there at the same rate; what changes is the fineness of the surface and the thinness of the fringe it resolves. Gravity is in tiles per second squared, so a wave runs at `sqrt(gravity)` tiles a second and nothing about the grid can alter that.
- **The mesh is the simulation's output.** Every column holding any water contributes a quad — the sheet FADES out as it thins rather than switching off at a threshold. A hard cutoff is invisible on a shoreline, where the fringe is a thin ring, and ruinous on a sheet that is that depth all over, which is what a raised tile holds: it punched holes in the sheet and you could see the tile through them. That went unnoticed for five commits because it took a SOLVER change to expose it — before hydrostatic reconstruction water evacuated a raised tile rather than sitting on it, so there was no thin sheet to put holes in. 329 columns ringed on all four sides by water they were not drawn with; none now. A quad's corners are the interpolated surface of the columns around it — averaging only WET neighbours, or the shoreline gets dragged down toward the ground. **A CORNER IS A CORNER**: every column meeting at one draws it at the same height, or the mesh comes apart. **And everything else about a corner follows that bed too** — how deep it is, how white, how fast, which way it leans — which for a long time it did not: those were averaged over both groups regardless, so the last corner of a sheet before a lip took much of its colour from the water at the foot of the cliff. That is the hard seam at the top of a waterfall, and it is not the arc. Measured at a brink with a foaming plunge pool under it, `shade` of the corner's own depth is 122 of 255 and the corner was drawn at 172, the extra fifty being the plunge pool's foam borrowed up the cliff — so the surface ended in a bright opaque lip while the sheet leaving it drew what the water on the lip actually is. The sheet is on the same curve now (`shade` of its own thickness, which at the lip IS the depth of the water on the lip) and takes its white from the same foam field the surface is painted from, so the two meet at **exactly** the same alpha: 111 against 111, 117 against 117, 115 against 115, where it was 235 against 172. It carries TWO values, split by the BED its contributors stand on — the average over those on the highest bed, and the average over the rest — because one corner can touch two bodies of water with nothing to do with each other, a sheet on a plateau and a lake at the foot of its cliff, and no single height serves both. **THE SURFACE LEANS INTO A LIP.** A free overfall does not go over the edge flat: the surface starts falling before the brink and is already tipped toward the fall when it gets there, and that drawdown is the curve you see at the TOP of a waterfall as opposed to the arc of the sheet below it. The solver has it — traced down a lip, 0.74, 0.70, 0.75, 0.75, 0.68, 0.65, 0.62, 0.63, 0.57, 0.48, 0.32 half steps, and the mesh draws every one faithfully — but it is 0.42 of a half step spread over two and a half tiles, SEVEN pixels of sag across a hundred and sixty-five, and a sheet cannot sag further than it is thick. What it can do is sag in the right PLACE: a real drawdown is steepest in the last fraction before the brink and the solver spreads it across cells because cells are what it has, so `DRAWDOWN` leans the same drop toward the lip over `BRINK_REACH` columns. It moves where the surface is DRAWN and not how much water there is, so the opacity — and the seam it closed — are untouched. **The honest limit is the depth: on a brink five pixels thick there is no rounded lip to be had at any price, and the curve has to come from the sheet.** **A FACE AT A LIP IS NOT HANDED OVER TO THE SHEET**, which was tried and was wrong. The argument for it was that a nappe hung from the surface occupies the height the face is drawn in, so the face is a pane of water over the air pocket behind a waterfall — but that conflates two things. The air pocket is below the BED, down the cliff, and a face never reaches there (`resolveSide` clamps it to the column's own floor). A face at a lip is the cross-section of the water standing ON the lip, and that water is really there; the sheet leaves from its top edge and drifts off the wall immediately, touching it along one line and nothing else. Faded out, what is left is bare cliff exactly as tall as the water is deep — nothing at a trickle, **82 pixels on a lip holding eleven half steps**. Like the fold in `render/nappe`, an error in proportion to the depth, invisible in every scene small enough to build by hand, which is why `brink` exists. **A LIP IS NOT A SHORELINE**, which is the third rule in that file and exists because the first two gave the water a lip to pour over and nothing told the FADE about it: a surface fades in over `SHOW_DEPTH` so a shoreline arrives instead of switching on, but water at a brink is thin because it is LEAVING — it accelerates over the edge, and the same continuity that thins a nappe thins the last column before one. Faded as though it were ending, a brink goes translucent and the ground shows through the one place a sheet has to be continuous: measured on a river over a twelve half step cliff, 908 of 11,520 lip samples drawn under full opacity, and every one of those 908 a column with a fall off it. `atBrink` reads the GROUND and the neighbour's SURFACE — no fall state, so the vertex shader can ask the same question, and so a still pool at a cliff edge answers yes too, its edge being a face rather than a shore — and the depth a column is SHOWN at is floored by it. On a stepped cascade that is 192 of 18,695 corners, the worst gaining 69 of 255 in opacity; on a gentle straight lip it is worth a third of one, which is the difference between a corner that averages with the deep water behind it and one that does not. It is a RAMP and not a yes or no, over the half of a fall's least height that ends at it: written as a threshold it was a threshold evaluated in double on the mesh builder and in float in the shader, and a hair either side flipped a whole column's opacity, parting the two paths' pictures by 35 of 255 where it happened. The rule lives in `render/corner-rule.ts` and nowhere else — along with the SIDE rule below, which is written in terms of it — because it was written out THREE times — once in the mesh builder, once in WGSL, once in GLSL — and by the third it was already wrong, which is what the hairline below was. There are two copies now and no more: `resolveCorner` is the rule in TypeScript, which the mesh builder calls and the tests pin down, and `cornerRuleSource` is the same rule as shader text, generated once and emitted in whichever dialect asks — so the two shader languages cannot disagree with each other at all, and a test proves it by reducing both to their skeleton and comparing. They sit a screen apart in one file, which is the only arrangement that makes a change to one an obvious omission in the other. **The two heights are ONE again wherever the lower water reaches the higher bed**, which is the same sill test the solver measures heads from: a body is separate when there is AIR between it and the other one's floor, and under a pool deep enough to drown a dip in the bed there is none. Split all the same, the columns either side of the dip get two heights for the same corner, differing only by the surface's own ripple — up to 0.57 half steps, with the LOWER group the higher one 39% of the time — and a corner that does not agree with itself is a crack. The CPU builder hides it under a side face, so it only shows on the vertex-shader path, where nine pixels of grass come through twenty half steps of water as a hairline along every dip. A bed belongs to a whole tile, so neighbouring columns always land in the same group and always draw the corner at the same height. It was a per-COLUMN threshold before — a corner far from a column's own surface was dropped in favour of that surface — and a threshold is a decision each column makes for itself: put a wave through a pond and the crest crosses it while the trough beside it does not, so the two stop sharing the corner between them and the sheet splits open. Nothing at rest, 2.3% of edges torn at a modest wave, 19% at a big one, gaps up to two hundred pixels of ground showing through the water. A corner is also never below the BED the column stands on, which is the one thing that cannot happen: over a one step rise the averaged corner is near enough to pass any threshold and still under the tile's own top face, so the sheet's edge sank into it. The sides of a body of water hang from the very corners the surface quad used, so the two share vertices and there is no seam, and a face on a tile's FAR edge is filed with the tile in front of it rather than its own. A face hangs DOWN from the surface, so one on a tile boundary pokes into the diamond of the tile ahead — whose terrain is a later band and paints over it. On flat water nothing shows, at any depth, because the tile ahead is at the same level and its own water covers the same strip; at a LIP it is six half steps down and covers nothing there, so what is left is ground showing through the sheet. Along a stepped cascade that is a row of square TEETH, one per band, each as tall as the water is deep — found by hiding one band's terrain at a time until exactly one tooth filled in. Only on the far edge, since an interior face never crosses a band boundary, and only where the ground ahead is BELOW the water: a tile in front that stands higher is genuinely in front, and its terrain covering the face is the band order doing its job — filed forward, a pond against a wall would paint its edge up the wall. The CPU builder simply puts the quad in the next band's batch; the vertex-shader path cannot, because a band's mesh IS its band and a slot may only choose what to draw, so it grew two more PARTS and each band draws the far-edge faces of the columns BEHIND it, which is the same sentence in the only grammar a procedural draw has. Those parts are idle for the three columns in four that are not on a boundary and idle again wherever the ground ahead is not lower, and an idle part collapses to a point: measured on a flooded 64² map, going from three parts to five cost 0.05ms of build and nothing at all that could be told apart in submit or wall time. and each end of a side reaches DOWN TO THE CORNER THE NEIGHBOUR ITSELF DRAWS, never below this column's own bed — and, where the neighbour is DRY, all the way to that bed whether the ground next door is lower or higher. Higher used to squash the face to nothing, the reasoning being that a face buried in the hillside next door is a face nobody can see. Two things made that false. **At the RIM OF THE MAP there is no neighbour at all** and so nothing to be buried in: the side gave up looking for one and returned, and water running to the edge was drawn as a sheet with a void under it, the terrain's own skirt showing through where the water's body should be — on every map, since running off the edge is the default. And in **X-RAY** the ground in front is translucent on purpose, so a face that was only ever hidden by it is a face you are now looking through at nothing. Drawn always, the normal view is unchanged, because the bank in front is a later band and paints over it; what changes is that the water has a body at every edge it has. Only WATER next door still raises the floor, and that one case is what the shorter rule was right about: a face between two halves of one body darkens the seam between them. **And the side takes the same opacity ramp the surface does**, on the depth of the water standing at that edge, which turned out to be the rest of the answer: the geometry was there all along — a flooded sixteen tile map builds a hundred and twenty-eight rim faces, each exactly as tall as the water is deep, covering the band between where the surface is drawn and where the ground is — and at the flat third of an alpha it used to have, what you saw through them was the grass. Measured rather than guessed: the faces span screen y 363 to 1023 while the surface stops at 891, so they cover the void exactly and always did. You see through the edge of a puddle and you do not see through the edge of a lake, which is both what water does and what makes a body read as a body; a one half step edge is unchanged at 0.46 against the old 0.48, and a four half step one goes to 0.92. That rule sits beside the corner rule and is generated the same way, in one TypeScript function and one shader template rather than three transcriptions; the tops come back already clamped to their own floors, so a side that should not be drawn at all is one whose two ends are both zero height — which the shader can draw regardless, since a zero-height quad makes no fragments, and which saves the mesh builder a quad it would only have to blank. The rule was "skip the side wherever the water next door reaches this column's bed, because then the two are one body and the surface covers the join" — a guess, and one that contradicts the corner split, which deliberately gives two beds two different heights for the same corner. Water running down a staircase tore along every step's seam: rasterised, 2.7% of the ground under interior water showed through, and every sample of it was on a tile with a one step drop, hard against that edge. Matched corner for corner to what the neighbour actually draws there is nothing left to leave showing — 0.1%, four samples of four and a half thousand, all of them exactly on a polygon boundary. Getting that wrong either way is expensive: a step's worth of height before drawing anything — left a shallow sheet on a raised tile hanging over it with nothing joining the two, a panel of water floating above the ground it was lying on; any height at all drew an edge wherever two neighbours differed by a hair, which on moving water is everywhere — sixty-eight thousand of them on a flat flooded map against sixty-five thousand pieces of surface. So the sheet rises, falls, tilts and retreats as the water does. **A current CARRIES a pattern** (`render/flow-wash.ts`), which is what makes it read as flowing — the solver knew the velocity of every column and the sheet lying on them ignored it, so a standing flow read as a still ribbon. The obvious way to draw one is a plane wave: bands across the flow whose phase is the corner's position projected onto the flow direction. It does not work and cannot be made to — the phase is `position · direction`, so two neighbouring columns forty tiles from the origin whose flow differs by eight degrees are four whole cycles apart. Measured, one column's shade correlated with its neighbour's at **0.002**: television static, which is exactly what it looked like. So the pattern is not computed from where a column is, it is carried. A still field of smooth mottling is what water looks like when nothing is happening to it; every frame the live field is traced back up the current, sampled, and pulled a little towards the still one. Moving water smears it into streaks along its own path and still water settles back. It cannot go noisy, because every value is an interpolation between values that were already smooth: the correlation is 0.79. Nothing in the still field is finer than a tile, either — at four columns to the tile a pattern the mesh cannot resolve is noise however coherent it started. **And the waves reveal it.** Shaded in proportion to their slope, a pond's ripples came out at four percent of the range and could not be seen, so what you got was a mottle sitting still while the mesh rippled underneath it — a texture painted on the world rather than on the water. The lean of the surface is read along BOTH axes now, since up-screen in this projection is up and left together, and through a response that is steep near flat and saturates, because the coefficient that makes a pond's ripples visible sends a river's standing waves off the end of the scale. The carried pattern is then shown where the water is moving AND where it is leaning, so a wave passing over still water brings the mottle out on its own face and lets it go behind — what you see travels with the wave although what it is made of stays where it is, which is how water works. A rippled pond went from a shade range of nine steps in thirty-one with one percent of its corners changing from one third of a second to the next, to twenty-six steps with forty-seven percent. Shades are looked up rather than computed: lightening a colour and packing it is thirty-odd operations and there are a quarter of a million corners a frame on a flooded map, so there is a table of shades of each fluid — thirty-two for the water itself and twenty-four more above them, for foam. The vertical edges are TWO things, and conflating them was a long-lived bug. The SIDE of a body of water runs from its surface to the ground it is standing on and no further — what is below that is rock, and running it down to whatever the NEIGHBOUR stood on painted a pond on a plateau over the whole cliff beneath it, worse the higher the ground was, because the excess was exactly the height of the drop. The FALL below a lip is drawn off the FLUX and not off the height difference: a height difference is a cliff, and a waterfall is water going over one. Both are full width at the top — the whole edge of the column is what the water leaves by, and inset from both sides they were a row of ribbons with the rock showing between them — and a fall narrows and fades below by how hard it is pouring and how far it has to go, since past a few half steps a fall is mostly air. A fall is drawn whether or not the column is deep enough to show a surface, because water accelerates and thins as it goes over a lip and at the lip itself it usually is not. **A fall is its own LAYER** (`render/falls-render.ts`), and not part of the water mesh at all, because it is the one thing on the map that is not where its column is. Every other quad in that mesh is a function of a column and therefore belongs to that column's band, which is the assumption the whole diagonal-band scheme rests on; water thrown off a lip travels TOWARD THE CAMERA as it falls and lands in a different tile from the one it left. Measured on a river over a twenty-four half step cliff, every one of 92 falls put its foot a band further forward than the band its quad was drawn in, and the terrain in front — a later band, with no depth buffer to arbitrate — painted over the bottom of all of them. So a fall is cut into pieces and each piece is filed under the band IT is in rather than the band its lip is in. **A QUAD BOUNDARY IS A BOUNDARY**, which is the corner rule again and failed the same way. A piece is one COLUMN wide, so its two lateral ends sit exactly where the next column's piece begins — and the pour and the throw are properties of a COLUMN, so taken from its own column alone each end disagrees with the one it is touching. Shaded apart that is a step running the whole length of the drop; thrown apart it is worse, because the two sheets then hang at different distances from the rock. Measured across the ninety shared boundaries of a river over a twenty-four half step cliff, neighbours differed by 0.03 of the opacity scale on average and stood 0.9 pixels apart, worst case 0.18 and thirteen pixels twelve half steps down — so a wide lip drew as a row of loose strips with cliff showing between them rather than as one falling sheet. Each end is averaged over the two columns that meet there instead, which is the same number computed from either side, exactly, because addition commutes. The OUTERMOST column of a run keeps its own value rather than averaging with the dry ground beside it: a fall is full width or it is bunting, and fading its edge to half is insetting every one of them by another name. **And past a few full steps it COMES APART.** A nappe is not stable: surface waves grow on it, the air drags at it, and it is thinning the whole way down because it is accelerating, so past some distance it stops being a sheet with a surface and becomes a great many drops travelling together. That distance is what decides whether a drop reads as a curtain hung off a ledge or as a waterfall. So past `BREAK` a fall sheds REAL drops — out of its own mass, through the same drip system a pipe uses, so the sheet is lighter for what it throws and `totalWater` never moves. They leave on the sheet's own arc, which is why `driftAt` lives in `fluid/falls` rather than with the drawing: a drop thrown two tiles while the sheet it left is drawn one tile out does not come off the sheet, it comes out of the cliff beside it. The rate is per EDGE and not a fraction of what is in the air, because it is the SURFACE of a nappe that comes apart and an edge is one column wide however much is going over it — a fraction of the volume would have a river shedding a hundred times what a trickle does. Even so a fall sheds along its whole WIDTH, so the rate that suits a fall off a notch asks for two thousand drops off one running the width of the map, and the list is a thousand long, past which a new drop is merged into an existing one and arrives somewhere it never was; backed off as the list fills, sixteen columns of river keep 127 drops in the air and two hundred keep 596, which is the right way round — nobody counts the drops in a curtain sixty tiles wide. What coming apart does to the DRAWING is take some of its body away and turn the rest white, those being two halves of one fact, since white water is white because it is full of air. Not all of its body: the first version faded the sheet out entirely and it was wrong the moment it was on screen — an eighteen half step fall stopped two half steps short of the floor and what replaced it was four drops. The bottom of a tall waterfall is the part you can see from furthest away. **And it PLUNGES**, which is the part that makes a waterfall do something to the water under it rather than be a picture hung in front of it. A fall used to arrive as `depth[to] += amount` — the whole of it, at rest, as though poured from a jug a hand's breadth above the surface — so the pool under a twenty-four half step drop was the same flat sheet as a pool under a tap, and everything that read as a plunge was foam painted on top of it and the odd drop shed off the sheet. It arrives at `sqrt(2 g h)` and that momentum turns at the bed and runs away radially; the DIVERGENCE of that is the standing depression you see under a waterfall, so it is not painted either — it comes out of the same continuity the rest of the solver runs on, along with the ring that travels away from it and the jump where that ring meets still water. Measured on a river over a twenty-four half step cliff: a pool 2.39 half steps deep flowing at 0.61 becomes 1.08 deep flowing at 1.48, with a rim of 2.25 in the column beside the hole. It is a FORCING and not an impulse, which is the one real difference from a drop's crater — a drop arrives once and what is capped is what that arrival may do, a sheet arrives every step for as long as the river runs and what is capped is the flow it BUILDS. Loosen that cap from the wave speed to three times it to nothing and the Froude number at the impact goes 1.05, 2.57, 3.27 while the pool is scoured from 1.06 half steps to 0.59 to 0.36: a waterfall that digs its own pool away. The push itself is exaggerated, and worth being exact about rather than calling it a fraction and quietly using four — at the honest value the depression is a quarter of a half step, which is both what the balance `q = gain * carry * head` predicts (0.30) and what real waterfalls do, and is a dimple nobody can see at any zoom this game is played at. Tripled it reads, and past that the cap takes over and it stops changing, which is the right place for a knob to stop. The PLUME is a garnish and not the mechanism, which took some getting right: it moves real water out of the impact and lands it a column or two away, so it digs a depression by itself, and in the first version of this it was digging most of one while the momentum contributed a fifth — a bad way round, because whether a plume becomes drops at all is a question about the length of a list, so a fall wide enough to exhaust it would have lost its plunge along with its spray. **And the foot of it goes white**, which it never did, because water arriving through a fall comes in the side door: it is added to the depth directly, and the solver's own breaking test reads the rate the SURFACE is changing, so the one piece of water on the map being hit hardest was the one piece that never churned. Two older things had to be fixed for any of it to be honest, both of them water climbing ground it could not reach, and both invisible until a fall started shedding its spray hard against a cliff. A crater is a raw flux written straight onto the edges rather than driven by a head, so it was the one thing on the map not subject to the sill everything else is measured from — drops landing in a plunge pool shoved water four hundredths of a unit UP a twenty half step rock face. And the landing test asks what the surface is under a drop and puts the water there, so a drop drifting past a cliff at half its height found the cliff TOP under it and landed on top of the cliff; a drop that hits a wall has hit a wall, and loses what it was carrying sideways and runs down the face. **The pieces are cut in TIME, not in depth, and biased toward the lip on top of that**, because a projectile's path is the straight one in time and all of its bend is in the first moment of it: cut evenly in depth a twenty-four half step drop put its first chord across 0.298 tiles of the 0.73 the whole fall covers — 41% of the bend, drawn as one straight line, at the lip, which is the one place a waterfall is visibly round. That is the hard corner at the top of a fall, and it is not a missing curve but a curve sampled where it is straight and straightened where it curves. In time the drift comes out evenly spaced because drift is `v t`, and the depths crowd toward the lip as `t^2`. Evenly in time is the right answer for how far a chord STRAYS from the arc, but what a lip looks like is an ANGLE, and the angle turns fastest at the very start: evenly in time the chords came out at 36, 50, 59, 65, 69 degrees on screen, so the first kink alone was fourteen. What turns evenly is spacing by the tangent angle, which is `t` proportional to `tan θ`, and a three-halves power is that shape without having to know the throw to work it out. **Measured on screen against the 33 degrees the surface arrives at and the 27 of flat water: the sheet used to leave at 62, and leaves at 29; the worst kink between chords was 18 degrees and is 7; no chord strays more than a tenth of a pixel from the arc it is drawn across.** It takes twenty-four pieces rather than six to get there, which on a real map — one river over one cliff, 88 falling edges — is 2,112 quads and 0.47ms in `drawFalls` against 528 and 0.12ms. A map that is nothing but staircase, every tread pouring, is the worst case at 27,120 quads and 4.5ms. **And `FALL_THROW` went from 1 to 3, and the water now lands where the arc takes it**, because a cap of one was throwing away most of the launch — the water at a lip is measured going 2.0 to 2.6 tiles a second — and a slow launch does not make a gentler arc, it makes a SHARPER one, since the parabola's width goes as the speed and its drop does not. The radius of the bend is `v^2 / g`, so it goes as the SQUARE of the launch while how far the water lands goes only as the first power — and traced off the drawn vertices the whole turn used to finish inside 24 pixels, less than one quad of the surface feeding it. The curve was the right shape at the wrong SIZE, which is why cutting it finer never helped. Measured, the turn to seventy degrees now takes 69 pixels against 38, and the sheet leaves at 28 degrees against the surface's 30. Bleeding the sideways speed off to keep the foot close was tried and does the opposite of what it looks like it should: taking the horizontal away while the vertical keeps building makes the sheet go vertical SOONER. So the foot goes where the arc goes and the water with it (`landsAt`), off a SMOOTHED launch — read raw, the landing point hops about with the flux and the pool comes out as a smear; smoothed on half a second, it sat on one column across every row and every sample. The cost is that a fall no longer lands against its own cliff, so its plunge pool is a dish in open water rather than a pit in a corner: 1.55 at the impact against 2.03 on the plain, where a wall-confined one was 1.06 against 2.15. The jet is still there and is now visible as a REVERSAL — the column upstream of the impact runs at −0.07 against a river flowing +0.70, which nothing but the plunge's own momentum can do. **And a sheet is white from the LIP**, not from `BREAK`: aeration used to begin four full steps down and nowhere sooner, so a shorter fall was drawn in the flat colour of the water that fed it, and next to a pool going white where it spills in and a plunge pool going white where it lands, the one part of the picture actually in the air was the one part with no white on it (0, 0, 0, 0, 0, 0 over the first eight half steps, against 0.04, 0.06, 0.10, 0.13, 0.15 now). It rides on the thinning, which already says how far along that road a piece is, and it CARRIES the foam that was on the lip — read off the solver's own `broke`, the same number `render/foam` paints the surface with, so a fall joins the foam above it to the foam below instead of interrupting them. What it is cut along is a parabola (`render/nappe.ts`): water going over a lip is a projectile and nothing more complicated than one, so at `b` half steps below the lip it has been in the air `sqrt(2b/g)` and is `v sqrt(2b/g)` out from the rock, and it is thinner there by the same factor it is faster — `1/sqrt(1 + b/4)`, from continuity, the same water passing every point of an accelerating sheet. That is what lets the sheet LEAVE THE WALL, which is the whole difference: hung on the edge plane it was IN the cliff, so it read as a decal, so it had to be faded out after four half steps, so most of a tall drop had no water drawn on it at all. **And the sheet hangs from the SURFACE, not from the bed.** It used to hang on `ground[i]` — the bottom of the water — so a body arrived at a lip, stopped dead in a vertical face as deep as it was, and a curtain of no thickness started again underneath: a sheer wall of flat colour along every brink on every map, and on a river over a twelve half step cliff, thirty-seven of them between four and nine half steps, up to 154 pixels of it standing on edge. The sheet is the body CARRYING ON over the edge, so where it leaves it is exactly as thick as the water standing on the lip, and the thinning above was the SHAPE of that thickness all along — it was being spent on opacity instead. At nought below the lip the top of the sheet IS the surface the mesh draws at the brink (measured live, 12.468 against 12.469), so there is nothing between them for a face to fill, and by four half steps the two have converged into the ribbon it always was. Per lip END and not per piece, exactly like the drift, since a quad's two lateral ends belong to two different columns' worth of lip. **And the height it leaves FROM is shared too**, which was the one lip quantity that was not. Pour, throw and thickness were all worked out from both columns so the two quads meeting at a boundary agree there; the lip was each quad's own `ground[i]`, so where a lip STEPS ALONG ITS OWN LENGTH — stairs with the water going off the side of them — a quad's far end and its neighbour's near end are the same corner drawn a whole tread apart. Measured on a six half step stair, 99 pixels of vertical tear at every step, with the cliff showing through it. Averaged, the curtain is one surface descending across the step instead of a plate per tread, and across 102 adjacent falling pairs not one of them disagrees by anything at all, because addition commutes. **A sheet is thrown along the FLOW, not along the edge it went over**, which is what makes a corner hold together. A convex corner is one column with a fall on both of its edges, and the far corner of that column is a point the two of them share — but thrown east and south respectively they shared it only where the drift was nought, and below that they pulled apart: a wedge of bare cliff down the whole height of the drop, widening the further it fell, with the water visibly stepping away from the corner on both sides. The water that made both sheets was travelling DIAGONALLY out over the corner, and thrown that way they land on the same line and the corner is a crease rather than a hole. A straight cliff does not move: there is no flow across it to speak of, so the across-component is nought. What it costs is that a piece is no longer exactly one column wide — where the flow along a lip differs between a piece's two ends, the piece stretches or squeezes by the difference, which is what a diverging flow does to a sheet: 1.3% of a column on average and 22% at its worst. Neighbours still MEET exactly, since the value at the boundary between them is still shared. The one thing clamped is how far it may be thrown — a river doing three tiles a second really does land two and a bit tiles out, and drawn, an arc that flat reads as water hurled at the valley rather than dropping into it, which is a worse lie than the vertical sheet it replaced. At one tile a second it lands about three quarters of a tile clear of the rock: plainly off the cliff, and plainly still falling. Built on the CPU, deliberately — the surface is a quarter of a million corners a frame and had to move to a vertex shader to be affordable, while a busy map has under a hundred falls and six pieces each, and keeping the parabola here means there is ONE copy of it rather than one per shading language, which is exactly how the falls came to behave differently on WebGL than on WebGPU. And a fall TAKES TIME to come down (`fluid/falls.ts`) — the WATER as well as the mesh. Water went over a lip and arrived at the bottom in the same frame, so a cliff was either bare or curtained with no moment in between: the wave at the top stopped and the wave at the bottom started, and nothing crossed the distance. A fall has a HEAD and a FRONT, both measured down from the lip and both accelerating under a gravity of its own — the solver's is a pressure gradient in tiles and has nothing to say about anything falling. While water is going over, the head stays at the lip and the front runs away from it, so a tongue of water reaches down the wall at the speed a thing falls: a twelve half-step drop takes 0.53s against a predicted 0.52s. When the flow stops the head lets go and falls too, so what is left detaches and drops away rather than being sucked back up; it clings for a sixth of a second first, because the flux over an edge flickers across zero and without that the fall stutters several times a second. The sheet thins with the distance fallen, so a long one is bright at the lip and gone before the floor. The water is IN THE AIR while it falls: it leaves the column at the top and is not in the one below until the front has gone the distance, and `totalWater` counts it, because a drop in the air is still a drop. **A fall whose cliff fills in LETS GO of that water, it does not drop it.** The branch that notices the drop has stopped being one used to land every unit in the air in a single step into a single cell, and on a real river that is tens of half steps, because an edge under a steady pour holds everything that went over during the time the fall takes. Measured on a twelve half step cliff, the cell below went from 6.7 deep to 27.7 in one frame — and it is self-sustaining, because the spike puts the pool up over the cliff, which is the very thing the branch tests, so the fall that would have replaced it dies the same way. The waterfall flickered several times a second and between flickers there was no sheet on the rock at all: 9 frames in 300 with a wet lip and nothing drawn over it. Drained over the time a fall of the shortest height there is would have taken, the same scene peaks at 6.0 instead of 27.7, gains at most 4.2 in a frame instead of 21.1, and never once shows a wet lip with no sheet. Off a twenty half-step cliff, nothing at all arrives below for the first 0.67 seconds — the time that drop takes — and the total reads 48.000 at every sample throughout. A fall is two terrain steps or more; one is a ledge water tumbles over and keeps flowing, and at a tenth of that threshold a steady hillside became a staircase of little cliffs with the water spending its whole journey in the air. A column with water in the air off one of its edges stays in the active box even when nothing is standing on it — dropped out, the fall stops being stepped and whatever is falling hangs there for ever, which is what happened to a waterfall the moment its shelf ran dry. Faces are FULL WIDTH top and bottom, so a run of them along an edge meets and reads as one sheet; tapered into the drop they were a row of wedges side by side, which looks like bunting. How much water is involved is in the OPACITY, and in how it shades from the top of a face to its foot. Both are only lightly aerated: whitened as hard as spray, the side of a still pool came out at RGB(171,199,215) against water's own (42,111,151), and a ring of near-white round every pool on a plateau is what made them read as panels stuck to the rock rather than water standing on it. Everything that used to be derived and drawn as decoration — pools, levels, waterfalls, chutes — is gone, because all of it was a consequence of solving waves on a flat plane per body of water, and a plane is a thing water cannot flow off.
- **Water goes WHITE where it breaks, and the white is a thing in its own right** (`render/foam.ts`). Foam is not a shade of water, it is something that happens to water and then sits on it: a crest breaks, and what it leaves drifts off downstream and fades long after the wave that made it has gone. Computed per frame from the surface — whiten whatever is steep right now — it reads as a highlight painted on the mesh, appearing and vanishing with the geometry underneath. So it is a FIELD, born in one place, carried by the current on the same backward trace the flow wash uses, and decaying on its own clock. **Where it is born is the SOLVER's answer, not this file's.** For a while it was both: this file carried three criteria of its own — a crest too tall for the water under it (the depth-limited index `H/h ≈ 0.78`, McCowan 1894), a front outrunning its own waves (the Froude number, which is the whole of open channel hydraulics), and a wave too steep to hold its shape (Stokes' limiting `H/L` of a seventh) — while the solver carried a fourth for the dissipation it does when a wave breaks. Two tests of the same thing in two places, and they did not agree: on a pour they fired on 46% and 58% of the water with 30% of it in common, and on a waterfall on 8% and 4% with 2% in common. So there was white where nothing was being dissipated, and dissipation with nothing to show for it. Breaking belongs to the solver because there it has consequences, and this file reads `broke`, the nought-to-one intensity the solver already works out — so the water goes white exactly where it is losing energy. It draws slightly MORE foam than the three criteria did, which was the surprise, and the worry beforehand had been the opposite: the solver's test needs the surface to be MOVING, so a steady standing wave should have gone dark. It does not, because nothing here is steady at column scale — a steady river comes out 35% white against 28%, a waterfall 44% against 31%, a big wave 22% against 17%, and a settled pond nought either way. It also took 329 lines out of this file and 1.3ms off the draw, because the tile-summary pass the steepness test needed went with it. What the retired criteria taught is worth keeping even though the code has gone. A crest test alone is blind on flat ground, and flat ground is where you pour — nothing on a flat sheet stands above the sheet. A Froude test alone is blind in still deep water. A steepness test asked at the GRID scale measures the scheme's own odd-even ripple and nothing else: a row through a disturbed basin read `8.34 7.36 8.18 7.44 8.07`, a zigzag of half a step per column, a slope of 0.49, whiter than Stokes, and the whole pond went white and stayed white. And any of them compared against neighbours has to be taught that a cliff is not a trough, or every raised puddle grows a white rim. **The one thing still decided here is the foot of a waterfall**, which is not a wave breaking at all — water arriving out of the air has air in it, and the solver has no opinion because landing water is put into the column directly rather than through the divergence its test reads. It needs a scale, though. Flat out, a walled basin grew a permanent white fringe: a film a thirtieth of a half step deep had crept onto the top of the wall and was trickling back in over a ten step drop, which IS a fall and does land, but lands a trickle. The edges of a fed waterfall carry two to five units in the air and that fringe carried 0.003 to 0.04, so landing foam is scaled by what is arriving. Foam is MIXED towards the white end of the ramp rather than added on top of the water's own shade. Added on, it had to start from wherever the lighting had got to — halfway up a ramp of thirty-two — so a fully broken crest landed at 40 of 55 and came out RGB(174) against water's own ceiling of 138: barely paler than water, for the whitest thing the renderer can draw. That was the whole of "I poured on a flat plane and there is no foam" — the foam was there, at 0.7 of full, and it was being drawn four shades above ordinary water. Mixed, foam of one is the top of the ramp whatever was underneath it. Foam whitens AND closes the surface: white water is full of air and air is not something you see through, and a crest that went pale but stayed as transparent as the pond behind it read as a highlight rather than as foam. It can only close what the water is already covering, gated by the same fade that brings a thin sheet in. A fed waterfall's plunge pool draws corners at RGB(210,225,233) against water's own (42,111,151), at 224 alpha where the water beside it is at 118.
- **The surface is a real mesh, not `Graphics`** (`render/quads.ts`). A quad batch per band writes vertices straight into one interleaved buffer — position plus a packed colour — and uploads only the bytes it filled, drawing them with a small flat-shaded shader. `Graphics` was tried first and its cost is not the triangles, it is REBUILDING: every frame it walks an instruction list, tessellates each polygon, recomputes bounds and uploads a whole new geometry. Measured on a flooded 64² map that was 100ms a frame against 1.5ms for the solver behind it, and it barely moved between 648 quads and 2564 — the signature of per-rebuild overhead rather than per-triangle work. The mesh does the same map in 2.2ms. Capacity grows by doubling and never shrinks, so a settled surface allocates nothing; quads dropped since the last frame are collapsed to a point, because the index buffer spans the whole capacity and anything left behind would keep drawing. `visible` is only touched when a band goes from having water to having none, since visibility is structural and flipping it rebuilds the scene's instruction list.
- **The mesh is built in a VERTEX SHADER** (`render/water-gpu.ts`). The CPU builder in `render/water.ts` is still here and still the reference the render tests hold to account — it is not dead code, it is the answer this path is checked against, and `?cpuwater=1` is how to get back to it. It began as a measurement of what moving the work would be worth. Of `drawWater`'s 9.15ms on a flooded 64² map, 3.29ms is working the corners out and 3.71ms is writing the vertices — 7ms of arithmetic whose whole life is to become 3.1MB of bytes the GPU reads once and the CPU throws away. The prototype computes them where they are used. The draw is PROCEDURAL: there is no per-frame vertex data at all, every band draws a fixed run of vertices, and each one works out from its own index which tile of the band it belongs to, which column of that tile and which corner of that column, then reads the simulation out of textures and computes its own position and colour. Per band because the scene is sorted by diagonal and one draw for the whole map would put every drop either in front of everything or behind it; a band is `x + y` in tiles, so its tiles are `(tx, b - tx)` over a range and that range is all a vertex needs to find itself. **Nothing is copied to upload it** — each texture is a view straight onto an array the solver already owns, which is why the state goes up as six single-channel textures rather than one packed four-channel one: packing would mean interleaving half a million floats a frame, which is the work this is meant to remove. **Which bands draw at all is the one thing this path has to be told**, and the CPU builder works it out for itself by walking the wet columns. Left out, every band drew its whole complement of vertices every frame however little water there was and however little of the map was on screen — and a map is mostly dry most of the time, which is the case that matters. Two ranges, intersected: the camera's, from the band renderer that was built around that question, and the water's, from the solver's active box — a band is `x + y` in tiles, so the box's corners bound which bands can hold anything. Measured across the range, both paths in one session on a 64² map: an empty map costs neither of them anything (0.00ms either way), a small pond costs the shader about 0.4ms MORE — the price of a procedural draw, which walks what could exist rather than what does — and a flooded map costs it **3.8ms against 26.9ms**, taking the whole water frame from 34.5ms to 11.3ms. That is with the sides and the falls drawn, which the first measurement of this was done without. What is left of the 5.25 is almost exactly the two carried fields, which are still the CPU's — the mesh build itself is near enough free. The browser figures are about 2.2× the headless ones throughout, because a preview that is not on screen is deprioritised; the ratio is the part to trust. It draws the whole surface now — the sheet, the SIDES of it and the FALLS over its lips — so the two paths put the same geometry on the screen. A column gets five quads rather than one: a procedural draw cannot skip, so a part that is not there collapses its four vertices onto a point and costs four vertex shader invocations and no fragments. That is the trade this path makes everywhere: the CPU builder walks what exists, this walks what could exist, and a nearly empty map costs it what a flooded one does. Everything both paths must agree about is imported from `water.ts` rather than copied, because two copies of the corner rule is exactly what put a hairline of grass along every dip in the bed on one path and not the other. Pixi will not generate a usable bind group layout for this on its own, either — it hardcodes every texture to `visibility: FRAGMENT` and `sampleType: "float"`, and these are read in the VERTEX stage from `r32float`, which WebGPU classes as unfilterable. Getting that wrong invalidates the pipeline, which invalidates the command buffer, which draws not merely no water but no world at all. **There is a WebGL2 version of the whole thing**, because a browser without WebGPU has to get water and not a blank map, and it is a transliteration on purpose: the same functions in the same order doing the same arithmetic, so a change to one is an obvious change to the other. `textureLoad` becomes `texelFetch`, `select(f, t, c)` becomes `c ? t : f`, and the uniform group arrives as plain uniforms rather than a struct. Two things had to be right and neither announces itself. The float textures must be NEAREST: `texelFetch` never filters, but WebGL2 will not call an `R32F` texture complete if it is asked to filter one without `OES_texture_float_linear`, and an incomplete texture reads as black. And the globals must be PLAIN UNIFORMS and not a `uniform globalUniforms { ... }` block — Pixi hands them to a WebGL program one at a time, its own shaders are built that way, and declared as a block nothing binds it: the driver says "used but unbound uniform buffer" and drops the draw. That second one had been true of the CPU mesh in `quads.ts` since the day it was written, so BOTH water paths drew nothing at all on WebGL and neither did anywhere else, because the editor asks for WebGPU and gets it. `?webgl=1` makes the other renderer one URL away, which is the only reason this was ever going to be found.
- **A NUMBER THAT CHANGES EVERY FRAME DOES NOT GO IN THE STORE** (`debug/water-meta.ts`). The wet count and the volume were store state, written from the tick whenever they changed — which on a flat map after one pour is every frame for as long as the puddle spreads. A store write is a React root pass, and in a profile of exactly that click React was the single biggest thing in the trace: **36% of it on the CPU path and 29% on the device path, against 16% and 7% for the entire Pixi tick**. Not in any component — every render function in the app came to single digits between them — but in `processRootScheduleInMicrotask`, React's fixed cost per commit, paid sixty times a second. Both numbers are a latch now, polled at four a second by the one small component that shows them, exactly as `perf.ts` and `gpu-water-stat` already were. The store is for what the EDITOR changes, and nobody clicks sixty times a second.
- **The solver's readback allocated 13.1MB to look at 0.43MB.** `getMappedRange().slice(0)` hands back the WHOLE staging buffer — the size of the packed state, every field, asked for or not — and copied all of it, every readback, plus the garbage that makes. It was the second largest thing in the trace. Only the runs actually asked for are copied now, as views into the mapped range, into arrays that live as long as the solver: **366ms to 4.5ms** in the profile after.
- **AND A CAUTION ABOUT READING A FLAMEGRAPH, paid for twice.** The cell readout's animation-frame callback came back at eleven per cent of the device trace, and it is not doing eleven per cent of anything: timed the way it really runs — once a frame, with the viewport's transform dirty again by then — `toScreen` is 6.7µs and the transform write 7.8µs, some six milliseconds over the whole trace rather than two hundred. What the sampler is charging to that frame is the main thread PARKED with it on the stack. It shows on the device path and not the CPU one, which points at the GPU queue and not at the tooltip. The same mistake as reading a serialised bench as a pass cost: the number is real, the attribution is not. Skipping the unchanged writes was still worth doing, and the CPU trace fell from 340ms to 9.5ms; the device trace's remaining two hundred is a stall wearing a function's name.
- **Nor does the POINTER go in the store** (`debug/pointer-at.ts`). `setPointer` fired on every pointer move with a fresh object, so every subscriber woke on every move — and `WorldScene`, the component that mounts the whole Pixi tree, was one of them. The crosshair it feeds is drawn from the tick now, beside everything else it overlays and only when the thing it draws has moved; the calibration panel polls. Two pointer moves and a hover change come to one store write where they used to come to three.
- **And the sidebar no longer subscribes to the whole store.** `useWorldStore()` with no selector re-renders on every write, and zustand hands back a new state object each time — so several hundred buttons re-rendered on every `setHover`, which is once per pointer move across the canvas. It names the slice it reads now.
- **THE GPU CAN BE ASKED WHAT IT SPENT, pass by pass** (`fluid/gpu/stamps.ts`, `world/render/device.ts`). The frame readout can only offer `frame - js` for the device's share, and that number is the GPU's work and the wait for vsync added together — at sixty frames a second it is almost all waiting and at forty it is almost all work, and it looks identical either way. `timestampWrites` on a compute pass answers directly, but the feature has to be asked for when the DEVICE is made, and Pixi makes its own with a hardcoded feature list. What Pixi does leave open is `options.gpu`: hand it an adapter and a device and it uses them. So the editor makes the device, with `timestamp-query` where it exists and the three texture-compression features Pixi wanted, and every pass is begun through `beginPass` so that switching it on times all of them and forgetting one is not possible.
- **THE WATER DRAWS THE QUADS IT HAS, NOT THE ONES IT MIGHT** (`render/quads-gpu.ts`). The mesh emitted five quads for every column of every band — 327,680 a frame on a 64² map — of which about sixty five thousand drew anything; the rest collapsed their four corners onto a point, which makes no fragments and costs four vertex invocations all the same. A compute pass now walks the columns, asks the same question the vertex shader asks, and writes the survivors into a per-band list; the band draws that many INSTANCES. Measured on `waterfall`: **61,212 quads instead of 327,680, and the render pass 14.5ms to 3.9** — 66fps to 104. The check that matters is `__waterCompare`, which puts the shader against the CPU mesh builder in pixels: nought differing out of forty thousand drawn, with the solver on and with it off.
- **Instanced, not indirect, and that is the whole of why it is small.** `geometry.instanceCount` lives on the geometry and both renderers read it at draw time, so the count can change per band per frame with no hook into Pixi at all — where an indirect draw needed the pass encoder, a dummy draw to carry the state, and a dead first quad to waste it on. The list is stored ONE HIGHER than the id so that nought means empty: the count is a readback and so a frame or two stale, the host rounds it up rather than risk drawing short, and the slots past the gathering have to collapse. Clearing the list is then all it takes to make them.
- **AND THE RENDER IS THE FRAME.** A render pass has to carry its own timestamps: bracketing it with empty compute passes of ours reads exactly nought, because submission order is about what the work SEES and not about when the hardware runs it — an empty pass that depends on nothing is free to run alongside the render rather than after it. So Pixi's own `beginRenderPass` is wrapped and the pair goes into the descriptor it was about to use. On the `waterfall` fixture the render pass is **16.7ms of GPU a frame** against a 15.5ms frame, and the whole solver is 3.4. The water was never what the frame was waiting for.
- **The first thing the instrument found was a cost this code had added itself.** `apply` came back at 5ms, seven times the rest of the solver put together, for arithmetic that is trivial — because every reduction in it is an atomic on a SINGLE slot, and an atomic on a single slot is a queue: sixty five thousand threads serialise on six addresses. Combined in workgroup memory first and handed up by one thread in sixty four it is 2.4ms, and the leak alarm it pays for is unchanged. The box's own min and max were doing this before anything was added to them.
- **And the answer overturned what the benches said.** The solver's whole compute is **about one millisecond of GPU a frame** — 0.97, 1.18 and 1.33 across three samples of the `waterfall` fixture, with the breaking diffusion the largest pass at 0.23-0.34 and nothing else over 0.15. The serialised bench had said the passes were the bulk of device time, because `onSubmittedWorkDone` after every frame measures the pipeline draining and not what a pass costs. The compute is not what a frame is waiting for, and it was about to be optimised on the strength of a number that meant something else.
- **A FRAME THAT IS SLOW IS STILL A FRAME** (`debug/perf.ts`). The readout dropped any frame over 50ms from its averages outright, to keep a wake-up after a tab sleep from swamping a rolling window of twenty. The trouble is that 50ms is three frames at sixty, so anything sustained under twenty frames a second had EVERY frame dropped — and a ring keeps whatever it last held, so the readout FROZE at the last twenty fast frames and stayed there, showing a healthy number while the thing in front of you stuttered. Silently, for ever, and exactly when a readout is most needed. It also made every measurement taken across a slow patch a measurement of the patch before it, which is worse than no measurement. A frame is now dropped only when it is long AND out of character — several times what the window has been seeing — so a wake-up after a second asleep still goes and a map that has settled into 55ms a frame is reported. Checked by hogging the main thread for 60ms a frame: the readout follows it down to 9fps and back up again, where before it did not move at all.
- **Where a frame's time went, top left of the world route** (`debug/perf.ts`, `debug/perf-hud.tsx`, DEV only). Four numbers, and it matters which is which: SOLVE is the water stepping — on the host path the biggest single cost in the frame and not drawing, on the device about a tenth of a millisecond; BUILD is turning the simulation into geometry, a handful of uniforms on the shader path and a quarter of a million corners on `?cpuwater=1`; PRESENT is the render call AND THE WAIT INSIDE IT — it hands the commands over but also blocks on the compositor having a buffer free, so on a quiet frame most of what it reads is idle; FRAME is the interval FPS is made of. **The last line is not a measurement and is labelled as such** — `frame - js` stands in: vsync when it sits near the refresh interval, the GPU behind when it does not. It used to say the GPU's own execution time could not be had, because `timestamp-query` must be asked for when a device is created and the device was Pixi's. **It is not Pixi's any more** (`render/device.ts` makes it and hands it over), and `__gpuTime()` reads the real per-pass cost; the estimate is kept because it is the one number available on every path, WebGL included. Rolling averages over twenty frames, because one frame is mostly whatever the garbage collector was doing. Measured around Pixi's own render by two ticker callbacks either side of its LOW-priority render step. **`present` is kept out of the `js` total on purpose, and the reason is the first thing this readout got wrong.** Read as a cost it looks alarming — 3.25ms on a nearly dry 64² map against a solve of 0.13 — and it sent me hunting a render cost that does not exist. Flooding the same map settles it: solve 0.13 → 8.94 and present 3.25 → **1.16**. It went DOWN as the frame filled up, which is the signature of slack and not of work. `__waterBench`, which drives frames flat out with no compositor to wait for, reads the same call at 0.5ms. What actually dominates a busy frame is the SOLVER: 18.7–20.5ms against a present of 0.5 on a flooded 64², which is forty to one.
- **The two paths are checked against each other in PIXELS** (`debug/water-compare.ts`, `window.__waterCompare()` in dev). The shader is the path that ships and the mesh builder is the one the render tests hold to account, which is the wrong way round unless something ties them together. The shared rules tie the DECISIONS together; this ties the pictures together. One scene with a dip in the bed under a deep pool, a plateau with a sheet on it beside a lake, and a lip for that sheet to go over — drawn twice into two render textures with nothing else in the frame, read back through the renderer's own extract, compared pixel for pixel. It is the only check that covers the arithmetic which can only live in a shader: the projection, the shading, the geometry of a fall. The two are allowed to differ, because the builder computes in doubles and the shader in floats and a corner half a millimetre apart in world space lands a fraction of a pixel apart on screen — so the measure is how MANY pixels differ and by how much. **Right, it reads: of 33,115 pixels either path draws, the worst single channel anywhere disagrees by 3 out of 255 and the mean by 0.4, nothing differs by more than 4, and neither path draws a pixel the other leaves empty — the same on both renderers, to the pixel.** Wrong, it reads 1,652 pixels differing, 5% of what was drawn, the worst by 248, with pixels on each side the other never drew: that is the corner merge put back the way it shipped, present in the builder and missing from the shader, because a comparison that cannot fail is worth nothing. It cannot run under `bun test`, which has no GPU at all, so it is a harness rather than a test — what a browser test runner would add is the running of it, not the substance.
- **The solver runs on the DEVICE, and that is the default** (`fluid/gpu/solver.ts`; the button top-left of `?world=1` turns it off, or `?cpuwater=1` pins the CPU). Fifteen passes in ONE command buffer, run in the order a frame runs them — the state's uniform block is a ring of sixteen slices at a 512-byte stride so a substep can write its own without waiting for a submit. **The compare harness diffs seven of them** (`?gpucheck`): diffuse, accelerate, limit, divergence, apply, falls, landings. Measured on the `waterfall` fixture through `__waterBench`, the frame is now vsync-locked at 8.3ms where it was 22-24; the solver's own compute is about 0.1ms of host time and 0.3ms of GPU, and what is left of the frame is the render.
- **THE DEVICE OWNS THE WATER, and the host sends a LIST of what it added.** This is the piece that took the port from slow to fast, and it is not a bandwidth trick. Sending the host's copy of the water up — whole arrays, or a difference of them — means both sides own it, and then every frame has to reconcile two versions of the state across a boundary three to five frames wide. Every water fault in the port came out of that reconciliation: pours wiped by a readback older than the upload that carried them, water lost at the edge of a region, totals drifting down as a puddle spread. A spring, a pipe, a brush and a landing drop now all record into `Arrivals` (`fluid/columns.ts`), which goes up as five floats per touched cell and has nothing to reconcile. What is still in flight is kept numbered and re-applied on top of whatever lands, so nothing is applied twice and nothing is missed.
- **And the surface is drawn out of the device's own memory** (`deviceSinks` in `render/water-gpu.ts`). Depth, both fluxes, the flow wash and the foam are filled by a buffer-to-texture copy on the end of the solver's own command buffer — same queue, so submission order is the ordering and there is no fence. Before that, every one of those textures was a view over a host array that Pixi re-uploaded every frame: a megabyte and a quarter going UP to say what the device had just sent DOWN. The draw went from 7.05ms a frame to 0.08ms. Ground stays a host upload (the device never writes terrain) and so does material (its texture is `r8unorm` and the field is a float; a buffer copy does not convert).
- **The LIPS come back as a list, not as seven maps** (`fluid/gpu/fallout.ts`). `air`, `front`, `head`, the two throws, and the flow wash and foam the surface is wearing, are arrays over the whole map — two and a half megabytes of the three and a half that came down every frame — and the only thing that reads any of them is `drawFalls`, which puts about five hundred quads on the screen. The transfer was three orders of magnitude larger than the thing it fed. The renderer is NOT what to change: its own header makes the case and the case is right — five hundred quads is not a budget worth a shader for, and the one parabola in `nappe.ts` staying the only copy is what stopped the falls behaving differently on WebGL than on WebGPU. So the arrays and the renderer stay and what changes is how the arrays are FILLED: a pass at the end of the frame writes the few hundred lips that have anything on them as eight floats each, and `scatter` clears the arrays and puts the list back into them. **3.63MB a frame to 1.17MB**, and the arrays come out bit-identical at every cell the renderer reads — checked by running both ways on one deterministic bench with `window.__fallList(false)`, and the check proved able to fail by taking the pass away with `__skip(["fallout"])`.
- **A pass of its own, not a few lines on the end of the falls pass**, and that is not tidiness: the falls pass returns early all over the place, so a row written from inside it would be missing for exactly the lips that had nothing to do this step — and a missing row is a stale row. The list is also COMPLETE rather than nearly complete, which rests on one fact: `drawFalls` reads a throw and a reach from the column beside each lip, but only ever from one `alongLip` has already put `falling` to, and anything falling is in the index.
- **The carried fields are NOT cleared, and they come back whole once in a while** (`CARRIED_BACK`, `CARRY_EVERY`). Two differences from the five per-edge arrays, both deliberate. The wash and the foam are not cleared before the list is scattered in, because a stale value in a column that is not a lip is read by nothing while a cleared one would leave the host holding a blank pattern. And they still come back whole every thirtieth readback — not because anything needs them per frame, but for THE HANDOVER: switch the solver off and `drawGpuWater` starts advecting the host's copy again and the textures go back to being views over it, so a copy that is minutes stale shows a visibly wrong pattern for the second it takes to re-settle. Half a second stale is invisible on a pattern that drifts this slowly, for a thirtieth of the bandwidth.
- **The lip row goes LAST in the frame, after the carried passes.** Written before them it carried the previous frame's wash — 240 lips out by a hundredth on a scale of one, which is small and wrong and exactly the kind of thing that is never found later. It was found by the both-ways diff, which is the argument for having one.
- **The FLUXES and the MATERIAL ride the lip row too, and the material texture is packed on the device** (`fluid/gpu/matpack.ts`). Nothing on the host reads a flux but `pourOf`, which asks about one edge of one lip — so one float carries what two whole maps did. Material was harder: its texture is `r8unorm`, one byte a column, while the solver holds it as a float, and a buffer copy does not convert — so it was the one texture of the six the device could not fill, which meant the host's copy had to stay current, which meant a quarter of a megabyte coming back to keep it so. A pass packs four materials into a word, which is exactly the bytes the texture wants in exactly the order it wants them, and the copy reads that. **3.63MB a frame to 0.43MB**, and the packed texture came back byte-identical to the device's own material across all 65,536 columns.
- **`canCopyOut` is asked PER TEXEL, not once for the layer.** The 256-byte row rule is about bytes, so it is a different question for a four-byte float than for a one-byte material: 256 columns gives 1024 and 256, both fine, while 128 gives 512 and 128 — the floats can still be copied and the material cannot. Asked once it came out as the stricter answer for everything and quietly took the five float textures off the device on any map narrower than 256. And the solver asks the SINKS whether material is being fed rather than keeping a flag, so if it is not, material goes back to coming down every frame — the two answers cannot drift apart.
- **And what goes UP goes only when it changes.** The ground and the splash marks were written to the device every frame — a quarter of a megabyte each, repeating what they said the frame before — and the ground texture was marked dirty for a third copy, because with the device filling the other five that loop had nothing else left to do. The ground carries a REVISION now (`ColumnField.groundRev`), bumped in `syncGround`, which is the one place the editor changes terrain under water; the splash goes up while any mark is alive AND ONE FRAME PAST THE LAST, because `splashed` turns false on the frame the last mark fades and the device is still holding what went up before it. Checked rather than reasoned, since a stale ground is a solver quietly running on terrain that has moved: raising a tile under a pool takes the ground 20 to 44 on the device and the water standing on it 7.9 to 0.017, pushed off the block it now stands on.
- **DEPTH comes back BY THE BAND** (`setDepthBand`). It is the one field that is neither a list nor a slow refresh, because it is not a rendering field — it is the water, and four host systems read it at cells nobody can predict: the pipes' physics at their mouths, the tile readout under the cursor, the wet count and volume, and the CPU solver the moment somebody switches back to it. No list serves all four. But the device only CHANGES depth inside the region it dispatched over, so every row outside that region is already right in the host's copy and copying it again says nothing. The region's rows cost what the WATER costs rather than what the map does: on the `lake` fixture 0.42MB a frame becomes 0.34, and on a flooded map it is all of it, which is the honest answer in both cases.
- **And the arrivals still in flight are put back ONLY inside the band**, which is the whole of what makes it correct. Outside it the host's own write is still standing — the scatter never reached that row — so adding the batch again counts the pour twice. Measured by sabotage: pour 60 columns from the water while a readback is thirty frames in the air, take the guard away, and 300 units of water read as 450. That sabotage also found the instrument's own gap — `checkPourLive` was only checking for water going MISSING, and called the surplus a pass. It checks both ways now.
- **The READOUT'S two numbers are counted where the water already is** (`fluid/gpu/meta.ts`). How many tiles are wet and how much water the map holds go in the corner of the editor, and the store worked both out by walking sixty five thousand columns AND a hundred and thirty thousand edges — every tick, for two integers. The device has the depths in registers while it is applying them, so it counts there: the columns per TILE (which is the grain the readout asks in — a tile is wet when the MEAN of its sixteen columns is over the dry depth, not when any one of them is) and the air per LIP in the same pass that writes the lip list, since air lives on lips and nowhere else. Both ride back in the reduction that already comes down. **1.10ms a frame to 0.043ms.** The wet count is exact; the volume is within three thousandths of a per cent, which is fixed point — WGSL has no float `atomicAdd` — with the add ROUNDED rather than truncated, because four thousand truncations all lean the same way and came out a hundredth of a per cent light every time.
- **And the LEAK ALARM is asked of the device too, which is the only place it was ever true** (`DEPTH_SLOT`, `AIR_SLOT`). The alarm's whole job is to say whether the device's own passes are losing water, so what it needs is the columns ALONE — the air is a different question answered by a different pass. Those two shared a slot, so the alarm could not be given the device's answer at all and summed the host's whole depth map instead: **43us a frame**, and worse, it stopped being true the day depth started coming back BY THE BAND, because the rows outside the band are the host's copy with pending pours in them. It was being shown a mixture and calling it the device's. Split into two slots it is exact, and the readout still gets the sum, which is one addition. Checked on a settled closed map with the springs off: 19473.3 against the host's 19473.2, five parts in a million, which is the fixed point.
- **The lip arrays are unwritten where they were WRITTEN, not everywhere** (`solver.ts`'s scatter). Five `fill`s over two megabytes a frame to unwrite seven hundred edges, and the list of which seven hundred was already in hand. The invariant is inductive — after a scatter the only non-zero entries are the ones it just wrote — so clearing exactly those leaves the arrays blank for the next one, with a full sweep on the first scatter and after a frame of `setFallList(false)`, which are the two moments something else may have written them. Its OWN record rather than `cliff`, which holds the same edges today but is a rendering list with two producers. 10.3us to 4.6us, which is a memset against scattered writes and the smaller win of the two — the point is that it now costs what the falls are rather than what the map is. Verified by sabotage, because "nothing is left behind" is not visible from the picture: flatten the cliff under a running waterfall so 732 lips become 134, and the real code leaves nothing outside the list while a build that forgets its record leaves 16 sheets hanging off a cliff that is no longer there.
- **THE WATERFALL SHEETS ARE BUILT WHERE THE WATER IS** (`fluid/gpu/sheet.ts`, drawn by `render/falls-gpu.ts`). `drawFalls` was two thirds of the host's mesh building — 0.765ms against the six MICROseconds `drawGpuWater` costs — and the difference was never that one is harder: the surface had stopped being built on the host and the sheets had not. Everything a sheet is made of is already in the packed field, which is why the readback carries a lip row at all, so the pass runs there and writes the finished VERTICES. **`build` 1.13ms to 0.27ms, `js` 1.50ms to 0.64ms.** The colour is a tint lookup rather than a mix, because the ramp texture already holds `aerate(base, paleAt(k))` for every shade of every fluid and the surface reads it the same way — so what the shader computes is the index, which is `sheetLook` minus the two tables on the end. Three things it cost, each of which looks like something else: `from` and `to` are RESERVED WORDS in WGSL, and a pipeline built from a shader that used them reports only that it was invalid all along; a staging buffer may not be MAPPED when a command buffer naming it is submitted, which silently discards the whole submission and reads as a solver doing nothing; and a vertex attribute's offset must be less than its ARRAY STRIDE, so the obvious shape — one buffer with a band at each offset — is an invalid pipeline that takes the entire render pass with it and leaves a blank map insisting it drew all 127 bands. A buffer per band and a copy into each, sized from last frame's count. Checked against the host, which is still the reference and still what runs on WebGL: the per-band quad counts agree EXACTLY across all 127 bands, and 94% of the quads match to the pixel, the rest being f32 against f64 either side of a rounding boundary. The COLOURS were checked too, and the answer is more interesting than "close": the sheet's cover — which goes through no table on either side — agrees to within 1 of 255 with a mean error of 0.02, so `sheetLook`'s arithmetic is the same arithmetic; and the RGB differs by at most 2 of 255 because this reads the shade ramp at an INTEGER index while the host evaluates `paleAt` at a fractional one. Measured over 260 corners: every device colour lands exactly on a ramp entry, only 42 of the host's do, and where both do they pick the same entry every time. That is a difference in the right direction — the surface quantises identically, so a sheet now takes its colour off the same ramp entry as the surface quad it leaves, rather than off an interpolation between two.
- **Timing a frame is not free, so most frames are not timed** (`Stamps.arm`). Two queries per pass, a resolve, a command buffer of its own, a submit and a mapped readback — every frame, for a readout that asks four times a second and averages over a window. Disarmed, `take` hands back nothing and there is nothing to resolve or submit; armed ten times a second the window still fills in a couple of seconds and the mean says the same thing. A harness that drives its own frames forces it, since a bench runs a fixed number of steps and a rate limit would leave most of them unmeasured. `GpuFrame.gpu` became a FUNCTION for the same reason: it was read off the clocks and stored on every frame for readers that ask when a human does.
- **What only an EDIT can change is worked out when the map is edited** (`Grid.rev` and `edited` in `world/grid.ts`). Six things walked the whole map every frame to find a handful of cells that had been sitting there since the last click: where the springs are (`runSources`), which cells carry pipe and which of them join into a run (`findPipeNets`), whether any cell is holding water for a pipe that has been deleted (`spillOrphaned`), how much is standing in the pipes and hanging at their mouths (`waterInPipes`, `waterAtMouths`), and the pipework the drip renderer draws. None of them can change on their own. The grid carries one REVISION now, bumped by every mutator, and each list remembers the revision it was built at — one counter and not one per layer, because an edit happens at the speed of a hand and one number to remember to bump is a great deal safer than nine. **89µs a frame to 0.7µs on the pipes fixture**, and the point is the scaling rather than the microseconds: every one of these was O(the map) to find O(the edits), so all of them grew with a map the springs on it do not. The failure mode is a list that is quietly wrong for ever, which nothing can notice from the inside, so every mutator is held to the bump by a test — and `source` got the setter it had never had, since a layer callers write by hand is an edit the map never hears about.
- **A splash mark is on a LIST, and the list is why the mark is real** (`fluid/drips.ts`). Fading them walked sixty five thousand columns every frame to touch the few hundred that had anything on them — and it ran on exactly the frames the map was busiest, since a mark only exists while water is arriving: **51µs to 0.05µs** on the same 66 marks, and 0.9µs at 459, which is the difference between costing what the map is and costing what is happening on it. The list is exactly the non-zero entries, so it also closed a real fault. A mark is three things that have to agree — the value, the list it fades from, and the flag the foam renderer and the device upload both gate on — and written by hand they did not: `plungeInto` set the value and left the flag alone, so the whiteness under every waterfall was invisible to the foam, was never faded because the fade returned early for the same reason, and then appeared all at once, at full strength and long after the water that made it, the first time some unrelated drop landed and turned the flag on. One door in, and it cannot happen.
- **The readback, end to end: 3.63MB a frame to 0.43MB**, and 0.33 on a map whose water is in one part of it.
- **THE LEAK WAS A DENORMAL SQUARED** (`fluid/gpu/accelerate.ts`). A pour on a flat closed map lost eight per cent of itself in twenty four frames, where the CPU lost nothing; on the live path, where the readback is thirty frames behind, it was ten. The bed drag divides by the carry SQUARED, and a carry of 1e-37 — an ordinary depth in water the wind is pushing into a dry cell, and measured at exactly that in this scene — squares to zero in an f32. With the flux at zero too that is 0/0, which is NaN, and the next pass takes `max(0, depth + NaN)`: zero on every device, so the cell goes dry and its water is gone. No error, no discontinuity, just a total that sags. The CPU does the same arithmetic in DOUBLE, where 1e-37 squared is an ordinary number, which is why a pass-by-pass comparison from the same state never saw it — and why the leak only ever appeared with the WIND on, since nothing else makes depths that small. Both solvers now conserve a pour exactly and agree on it to the last bit.
- **A conservation instrument, because the fault was invisible from outside** (`CLAMP_SLOT`, `DELTA_SLOT` in `fluid/gpu/state.ts`, read back in `GpuFrame.reduce`). The clamp counts what it eats and the divergence sums every delta it writes; both should read nought, and a frame where either does not is creating or destroying water in the arithmetic. It was the delta sum that found this one — it came back saturated at 2^31, which is an `i32` conversion of an infinity. Alongside: `__pourCheck` prints the puddle as a PICTURE, both sides, because counts describe a fault and only a shape diagnoses one. And `__readback`, `__wholeMap` and `__skip` take the readback, the dispatch region and individual passes away, which is how the region and the falls were ruled out — bit-identical answers with the region forced to the whole map.
- **The leak alarm is on the readout, and it LATCHES** (`debug/gpu-water-stat.ts`, under the water toggle). Two readings: `held` is every depth the device holds, summed out of the readback, which on a closed map with nothing pouring is a constant — anybody watching it drift is watching a bug; `flux` is every delta the divergence wrote, summed, which is nought on a closed map and on an open or cliffed one is the water leaving the columns, so it is a reading rather than an alarm. The alarm itself is the CLAMP: `max(0, depth + delta)` should never have anything to do, and when it does, water is being destroyed. It gets its own red line because the fault it watches for is silent by construction and a number that goes wrong inside a grey block of eight others is one nobody reads — and it latches, because a bad frame at sixty a second is a flicker nobody sees. `__skip(["limit"])` is the sabotage that proves it fires: it does, within a second, and clears when the solver is rebuilt.
- **And the substep's weather needed a ring of its own.** The wind gusts on the clock and the drag is raised to the substep's own dt, so both are rewritten every substep — safe when every substep was its own command buffer, and not safe once the frame became one, because `queue.writeBuffer` takes effect where it is CALLED in the queue. Every write landed before the first dispatch ran, so every substep read the last one's weather. They now ride the same sixteen-slot ring the constants do, and the slot is folded into the OFFSET the constants already carry, so no shader knows about it.
- **THE WEBGL PATH HAD NOT DRAWN FOR A LONG TIME, and nothing said so** (`world-scene.tsx`, `render/water-gpu.ts`). It is the path somebody without WebGPU lands on, and `world-editor` says in as many words that it "rots quietly until somebody opens the page in a browser that has no WebGPU and gets a blank map" — it is one URL away (`?webgl=1`) and nobody had opened it. Four faults, each hiding the next. **The scene did not mount at all**: `encoder?.beginRenderPass.bind(encoder)` guards the encoder being absent and not the METHOD being absent, and only WebGPU's encoder has that method, so it threw inside an effect during mount and took `WorldScene` down with it. **Then the water shader did not compile**: WGSL's `select` is generic, GLSL's functions are not, and only the float overload existed — so `select(0, 1, k < 2)` on ints, which the corner rule writes to pick a neighbour offset, found no signature and cascaded into a dozen type errors. **Then a whole-number constant**: `RIM` is 1, and GLSL refuses `1 * aFloat` where WGSL promotes it. **Then it compiled and drew nothing**: the vertex shader looked its quad up in an `r32uint` texture through a `usampler2D`, which this path could not sample — and it never needed to, because gathering is a compute pass and WebGL has none, so the list there is always the identity and the answer is simply the instance index. Now verified the same way the WebGPU path is: `__waterCompare` on WebGL reads **39,544 pixels drawn, 0 differing**, at 97fps. Three static guards were added for the two traps a string can be asked about — a `select` overload per type the shared rules use, no bare integer multiplied into a float, and nothing named for a GLSL keyword, because `flat` is an interpolation qualifier there and a local named for it is a syntax error rather than a warning.
- **THE DEVICE SOLVER IS THE DEFAULT NOW** (`state/world.store.ts`). It was off, and the reason was a good one: the host solver is the REFERENCE — what the render tests hold to account and what every comparison is measured against — so putting a map on the device path without anybody asking meant water nobody had chosen. What changed is that the two are held to each other rather than taken on trust: `__waterCompare` diffs the paths pixel for pixel, `?gpucheck` diffs the solvers pass by pass, and the reference is one toggle away. What it buys is the difference between a map that is pleasant at any size and one that is not — on a settling 128² map the host solver spends about 14ms a frame against the device's 0.2, and the gap is all in the first seconds after a generate, which is exactly when somebody is looking. It DEGRADES rather than fails: with no WebGPU, a device that never arrived, or one that has been lost, the effect that builds the solver falls straight through to the host path and the toggle says so in as many words — `water: cpu (no device)`.
- **THE DEVICE FILLED ONE STOREY OF THE SURFACE'S TEXTURES AND THE REST STAYED AT ZERO** (`fluid/gpu/state.ts`, `copyOut`). Water on a bridge was not drawn at all on the device path. It was solved correctly, read back correctly, and labelled into the right sheet — `__deckBodies` on a live map: 136 wet deck columns, 136 with an id, none missing — and it reached no pixel. Every per-slot field is a stack of planes, and the textures the renderer samples are built that tall: `nx × ny·layers` for the depths and the material, `nx × ny·layers²` for the fluxes. The buffer-to-texture copy asked for `ny` rows of each. So on a map with a bridge the deck's plane of the depth texture was never written by the device at all; it held the zeros it was created with, the shader read every column on every span as dry, and dropped the quad. It takes the rows off the field's own length now, so nothing has to be kept in step with the table of shapes, and at one layer every one of them is the `ny` it used to be. **What made it hard to find is that every check that uploads the depths from the HOST's copy draws it perfectly** — the offscreen render of the same map reported the deck's water at 180 pixels a column while the screen showed a dry bridge, because the check was filling the texture the host's way and the game was filling it the device's way. `__liveWaterPixels`, which renders the running app twice and subtracts, is what found it: 187 units of water on the deck, and removing all of it changed **zero pixels**.
- **EVERY PROBE FOR THE BRIDGES READ THE HOST'S COPY, AND THE SHADER DRAWS THE DEVICE'S** (`world/debug/device-read.ts`, `window.__deviceIds()` and `__watchDeviceIds()` in dev). The picture on a storeyed map is assembled from sources that are not the same water: the device's depth texture, which is what the vertex shader samples; the host's copy of it, which the readback leaves a band behind and, on a sparse frame, up to thirty readbacks behind; and the sheet ids, computed on the host FROM that copy every frame by `findBodies` and uploaded for the shader to group corners by. **Every instrument written for this compared the second against the third, which agree by construction** — the ids are the copy, classified — so `__deckBodies` reported `noBody: 0` and `__watchDeckHoles` reported `worstHoles: 0` on a span with a hole visibly in it, and each true number sent the fix into a layer that was not wrong. This reads the depth texture and the body texture straight back off the device and counts the pair nobody could see: columns wet on the DEVICE with no id, which the shader draws as pseudo-sheet `-1` alongside every other unlabelled column at the corner, at the wrong height and alpha; and columns labelled for water the device no longer has. It reports the same two counts against the host mirror beside them, so the difference between the instruments is legible in one reading rather than argued about. Its staging buffer is its own, so the 256-byte row rule is padding here and not a constraint on the map — `canCopyOut` says a 96-column map cannot take a copy IN, and this has to be pointed at exactly those maps. Two things it must not get wrong, and both were wrong first: the ids are COPIED before the readback is awaited, because `drawGpuWater` rewrites them in place every frame and a reference held across three frames of waiting compares this frame's depth with a later frame's ids and invents the staleness it is measuring; and the watch takes one sample before it starts and abandons the run if that sample is not an account at all, because a watch that reads "the host is solving" as a hundred and fifty clean frames is the exact failure this was built to end.
- **AND THE BARE FRAME THE PIXEL PROBES SUBTRACT NOW TAKES THE WATER OFF BOTH SOURCES** (`world/debug/device-read.ts`, `silenceDecks`). `__liveWaterPixels` and `__watchDeckPixels` measure the deck's water by rendering the live scene with it and without it and counting what changed. Without it meant zeroing the host's upper storeys and redrawing, on the reasoning that the columns would go `NO_BODY` and the builder would drop them. They do go `NO_BODY`, and **the GPU has no skip for it** — it draws such a column as sheet `-1` — and on a carried frame nothing uploads the host's depths at all, so the texture being sampled still held every drop. The reference contained the thing it was the reference for, which makes a difference smaller than the truth and a hole harder to see the longer you look. It writes zeros into the device's own plane now and puts the read-back depths back afterwards, with nothing allowed to yield in between: the solver's next `copyOut` would otherwise put the water back mid-measurement.
- **THE GAP WHERE A ROAD MEETS A SPAN WAS THE DRAWDOWN LEANING INTO A HOLE IT COULD NOT REACH** (`render/corner-rule.ts`). Water at a brink is drawn thinner and lower, because that is what water about to go over an edge does — and `atBrink` decided where the edges were by comparing bare GROUND within a storey. At the mouth of a bridge the road stands level with the deck and the channel runs fourteen below it, so the road read as a lip: the surface was leaned into a hole the water cannot get to, and the corner height dived from 2.50 to 1.36 over four columns while the deck beside it sat at 2.2. That trough, at both ends of every span, is the gap somebody sees where a road meets a bridge. The abutment is CLOSED — it is a slot roofed below the road's own floor, which is what an abutment is — and the solver has known that since slots existed. The renderer was asking a different question, which is the same fault as every other seam in this water: one rule written twice. It asks `connected` now, the solver's own, in both dialects; the surface runs 2.48 → 2.51 → 2.47 → 2.44 across the join and on down the span. A real lip, off the SIDE of a deck into open air, still draws down — there is a test for each, and taking the rule out fails the first one.
- **AND A MAP WITH BRIDGES ON IT RUNS THERE TOO NOW** (`fluid/gpu/solver.ts`, `fluid/gpu/landings.ts`). Every pass walks slot pairs, but what held the device back was not a pass: the depth readback asked for one plane's worth of rows and the device holds `cells * layers` of them, so every column on a deck kept whatever the host put there before the device ever ran. It reads exactly like the device inventing water and it is the opposite — the trace prints the device's own tally beside what came back for that reason, and it sat flat at 3627.99 for twelve frames while the mirror climbed to 3634.1. Three more of the same shape hid at one storey, where a column index, a slot index and a pair-edge index are all the same number: a per-column write flag that silently dropped every pour onto a bridge, an active box grown from a slot index into rows that do not exist, and a lip scatter using one of the three for all three. **And the landings pass had been racing itself since it was written** — each invocation cleared its own edges in the dispatch where it wrote its neighbour's, and an edge has two authors doing an unsynchronised read-add-write on one float. It needs water landing in two columns that share an edge before it can fire, which no scene had ever asked for; the backward half gathers now. Measured on a live bridged map: **18.2ms a frame to 8.3ms**.
- **TWO BRIDGED SCENES, BECAUSE ONE CANNOT DO IT** (`fluid/gpu/compare-pass.ts`). At one storey every slot loop runs once on pair (0,0), so no scene that existed said anything about the arithmetic that is new. Building one that does is harder than it looks, because every drop height sits on a threshold: under four the parapet is not a cliff, AT four the two sides land either side of `FALL_MIN` by a thousandth and one stops calling it a cliff at all (which is what a 1.5% difference in the air turned out to be), four to eight lands a sheet and a landing throws a crown whose star turns off a counter the host advances in lip order and the device by atomic claim, and over eight the sheet breaks and sheds as well. So `spannedDry` has no drop at all: the channel runs FULL, the puddle leaves the deck over the parapet as head-driven flux across a (1, 0) pair — the most slot-specific thing that can happen on a map — and the culvert is pressurised while it does. It is judged tight and agrees to 1.5e-3 of the deepest column at 150 frames with the volume at 1.9e-9. `spanned` keeps the falls and the spray and is judged by the bounds that scene's accepted drop-ordering difference earns it. Falls over slots are held tight by the per-pass comparisons, which run on `spanned` and agree to a part in ten million at every settle from thirty frames to two hundred and fifty.
- **THE QUAD LIST WAS TWICE THE SIZE IT COULD EVER USE** (`render/water-gpu.ts`). It is indexed `[slot, band]`, and the stride was the WIDEST band's worst case — every tile on the longest diagonal, all sixteen sub-columns, all five parts — allocated for every band. But a band on a corner holds one tile's worth and the middle band holds `min(w, h)`, so the rectangle came to `min(w,h) * (w+h-1) * 80` slots against the `w * h * 80` quads that can exist anywhere on the map at once. Measured at three sizes it is **1.99× in every case**: half of every allocation was unreachable by construction, flooded map or not. Given its own offset each band takes what it needs — **9.96 MB → 5.00 MB at 128², 2.48 → 1.25 at 64²** — and because the list is then a flat thing stored two-dimensionally, its texture WIDTH is a number chosen for the copy rather than one the map dictates. That removes a second, quieter bug with it: the copy's row must be a multiple of 256 bytes, and it used to be the stride, so a map whose shorter side was not a multiple of four **lost its gathering outright** and every band drew its whole complement for ever — correctly and slowly, which is why nobody noticed. Verified with `__waterCompare`: 39,544 pixels drawn, **0 differing**, nothing drawn by one path and not the other.
- **AND THE MAP SIZE IS A SLIDER, which is what turned all of that up.** It runs to 128 tiles — 16,384 cells and 262,144 water columns, 119fps on the device solver and 102 on the host one. It briefly stopped at 96, and the reason recorded for it was wrong: a `RangeError` at boot looked like it scaled with the band count, and it does not — it happens at 64² as well, on a fresh load, and has nothing to do with the size of the map. The one real ceiling was the quad list's texture width, above. Worth writing down because a wrong reason in a comment is worse than no comment: it stops the next person measuring.
- **A DEVICE GETS THE GUARANTEED LIMITS UNLESS IT ASKS** (`render/device.ts`). 8,192 across for a 2D texture, however much the hardware can do — this adapter offers 16,384. The quad list's old width wanted 10,240 at 128 tiles, so the texture came back invalid, Pixi bound it anyway, and every frame after was `is invalid due to a previous error`. Asking for what the adapter already has costs nothing and cannot fail.
- **THE FIRST ERROR IS THE ONLY ONE WORTH READING, AND IT WAS THE ONE YOU COULD NEVER SEE** (`render/device.ts`). WebGPU poisons everything downstream of a mistake, and each poisoned thing re-reports itself once a frame at sixty a second — within a second the console holds thousands of messages saying only that something earlier went wrong, and the one that named the fault has scrolled out of the buffer. The first real error is now kept and shouted and the cascade is counted, and `?gpuwhy=1` wraps every creating call on the device in its own error scope, which catches a failure where it happens and with the descriptor's label in hand. Pixi builds its pipelines inside its own scope and caches them, so without it a bad pipeline is invisible at the moment it is made and cannot be provoked into failing again.
- **A frame that does not VALIDATE does not run, and says nothing about it.** A compute pass whose bind group does not match its layout poisons the encoder — `finish` hands back an invalid command buffer and the whole frame is dropped — so the water sits exactly where the last upload left it, which reads as a settled map rather than as a bug. It cost a session of measuring nothing happening. The first four frames of every solver now run inside a validation error scope and go to the console if they fail, and `GpuFrame.reduce` carries the reduction raw, because the host keeps a box of its own that `scatter` merges in and a reduction that came back at its seed looked exactly like a map with no water in it.
- **Water is live state, not a map layer.** Depth is a float that changes every frame, so it sits outside the grid the way the road network does: pouring is undoable, the flowing is not. The `fluid` layer records what was poured where, for saving a map; the depth is the simulation's.
- **A PIPE is a rate on a FACE, and it drips** (`fluid/drips.ts`, `world/water/pipes.ts`, drawn by `render/drips-gpu.ts`). A spring wells up out of the ground it is on; a pipe sticks out of a wall, and the side it sticks out of decides where the water goes — so it is a cell plus a facing rather than a third kind of source. **What makes a drip a drip** is the one piece of real physics here: a drop hanging at a nozzle grows until its weight beats the surface tension holding it on, and lets go at about the same size every time, because both sides of that balance are fixed by the nozzle — Tate's law, `V = pi d sigma / rho g`. The useful part is that the volume is a CONSTANT, so a mouth accumulates and releases one whenever it has a drop's worth: slow flow drips slowly and regularly, fast flow releases them faster than they can separate, which is a stream, and the change between the two is not a mode anyone had to write. The SIZE is a game quantity and the file says so — a real drop is four millimetres across, which over a two metre tile is a depth of eight nanometres, invisible and always would be. What is faithful is the shape: accumulate, detach at a fixed size, fall freely, land, splash. A drop is really in the air — it leaves the mouth, it is not in the column below until it arrives, and `totalWater` counts it in between, because a drop that vanished for half a second on the way down would be a leak. What is in the air falls whether or not anything on the map is WET, which is the one case that did not work: a step over a dry map has no active region and used to return before the drops moved, so a pipe over dry ground put twenty of them in the air in five seconds and landed none — and an unrelated puddle five tiles away fixed it, which is the tell. A pipe is often the first water a map ever gets. It lands on the SURFACE and not the bed, so a pipe over a filling pool has a shorter fall as the pool comes up to meet it, and it leaves a mark for the foam: a drop arrives through `addWater` rather than through the divergence, so the surface rate the breaking test reads never sees it. The mouth hangs just BEYOND the face, over the neighbour's own edge column, because that is where something bolted to a wall is; on the rim of the map it drips over its own edge, which is the honest answer for a mouth pointing at nothing. Placed pointing at the LOWEST neighbour — a pipe pointing into a hillside is not a thing anyone means to place — and clicking a cell that already has one turns it to the next facing, which is the only way to say "not that side" with one tool. Placed pointing at a face you can SEE where nothing is lower: all four neighbours tie on flat ground, so the tie-break is the whole answer, and N and E point away from the camera — defaulting to N put every pipe behind its own tile. **A drop RINGS.** One that has just pinched off is stretched, not round, and surface tension is a restoring force: it oscillates prolate to oblate and back at the Rayleigh frequency, `omega^2 = n(n-1)(n+2) sigma / rho a^3`, dying away as it goes. The rate is slowed to something the eye can follow — a real four millimetre drop rings at about fifty hertz, which at sixty frames a second is a blur — but the SCALING is kept: frequency goes as `a^-3/2` and volume as `a^3`, so a fleck thrown off a crown shivers faster than the drop that made it without anything having to say so. **A drop LANDS on the water rather than in it** (`splashInto` in `fluid/columns.ts`). It arrives with `m v` of downward momentum and the surface has to do something with it; what it does is get out of the way. Vertical velocity is not a variable a shallow water scheme HAS — that is the shallow water assumption — so the momentum cannot be handed over as itself, but its consequence can: an outward flux on the four edges of the column that was hit, which is a crater. Nobody draws what follows. The crater's walls stand proud, gravity pulls them back and past, and the disturbance runs outward at the wave speed — a ring, made of the same quads everything else is. Measured frame by frame on a six deep pond, the middle runs +0.15 (the drop's own mound), then −0.31 (the crater), then +0.39 — a rebound nobody put in, which is the Worthington jet — with the ring in antiphase throughout. The shove is capped at half the local `sqrt(gravity * depth)`, because a crater is an initial condition the solver has to be able to carry and asking a scheme with a Courant limit for something supersonic gets ringing instead. Only water with room for a crater gets one: a drop on dry ground or on a film just wets it. **And past a threshold it throws a CROWN.** The real criterion is a Weber number, `We = rho v^2 d / sigma`, of about forty — below it the drop merges with a ripple and nothing leaves the surface, above it the crater's rim goes unstable and flings droplets off. With the drop size fixed by Tate's law that is a speed, so that is what it is. The solver decides how much spray a landing is worth, because only it knows how deep the water is, and hands the volume back to be put in the air as drops — which are drops like any other, and land, and are counted the whole way. Beware the units there: a fall is in HALF STEPS a second and travel across the map is in COLUMNS a second, and thrown raw an impact at fifty flung flecks at fourteen columns a second and the crown came down three tiles away, which on a cliff is a steady drizzle landing back on the clifftop above the waterfall. The projection draws a half step at half a column, so that is the rate one converts to the other at, and a crown now lands about a column out — which is what a crown does. **Drawn on the GPU as a SILHOUETTE, not a rectangle** (`render/drips-gpu.ts`), which is what it used to be and you could see it. Every site is a closed outline built in the vertex shader as a fan of fourteen points around a parametric curve, and which curve depends on what it is. A free drop is the teardrop, `x = cos t`, `y = sin t sin^m(t/2)`, cusp TRAILING the travel — at `m = 0` that is exactly a circle, which is what a drop at rest wants to be, and the exponent draws the tail out as it picks up speed. A hanging drop is the same curve with the cusp pointing up into the mouth and the exponent driven by how full it is, which is the NECK: it grows nearly round and then, in the last of it, the neck thins and goes. The hanging drop was there all along — it is the mouth's own pending volume, the same number Tate's law is counted in — and what was missing was anywhere for a renderer to look it up, which is the difference between a pipe that emits beads and a pipe that drips. The fitting is the same machinery with the outline pushed out to a rectangle; it runs from inside the wall out to the MOUTH, because the two ends are the two things that have to line up and a fitting that stopped at the face left the drop hanging half a column clear of the pipe it was supposed to be coming out of. Shading is per fragment off the silhouette: the outline gives x and y in the drop's own frame and the front of the drop is whatever is left of the unit radius, which is enough for a normal, and a normal is enough for the highlight the eye is actually looking for — a drop is a lens, and the one thing everybody has seen a drop do is catch one. The streak is kept honestly separate from the ringing, because they are different claims: the ringing is what the drop IS, the streak is motion blur drawn as geometry, since a real drop at these speeds stays round and what makes rain look like lines is an exposure. State goes up as one small float texture, one row per band, so a band's mesh reads its own row and no uniform changes after the layer is built; bands with nothing on them are switched off, so a map with one pipe on it pays for one band. **Both shader dialects are generated from ONE text** — the WGSL and the GLSL2 differ by a table of tokens and nothing else, and a test reduces each to its skeleton and proves it. The corner rule already had to learn this the hard way. And the bind group layout must declare Pixi's own groups VERTEX and FRAGMENT even though this shader only reads them in the vertex stage: Pixi builds one bind group for the globals and hands it to every pipeline in the frame, and a layout that disagrees about anything, visibility included, does not merely fail to bind — the device rejects the draw, which invalidates the command buffer, which throws away every other draw encoded alongside it. What that looks like is a completely black map with the overlay insisting it has drawn every band, because it has, into a command buffer nobody submitted.
- **Pipes that TOUCH are one pipe, and every opening is the same rule** (`world/water/pipes.ts`, `world/water/pipe-net.ts`). Adjacency, the same connection rule roads use, so a run you paint is a run that carries; two runs that pass without touching are two networks, and a flood fill finds them from the grid rather than carrying an id, because the networks are a view of the map and the map is what the editor changes — so the fill runs on the EDIT that changes them and not on every step (see below). The water is held PER CELL. Booked against a network it would have to be divided on every cut and summed on every join — bookkeeping with no right answer, since nothing records which half it was in — and held per cell there is no question to ask: cut a run and each side keeps what was standing in it. **There is no inlet and no outlet**, and no direction of flow written down anywhere. A port compares the level inside the pipe with the surface of whatever is outside it and moves water down the difference, Torricelli through an orifice at a `Cd` of 0.62 — which is not a tuning knob but the vena contracta, the jet necking down past the hole, measured the same since the eighteenth century. Everything anyone wants out of a drain falls out of that one signed number: an end under a pool DRAWS because the pool stands above it, an end in the air DISCHARGES through the same mouth the drips already come out of so it drips and splashes and foams as before, and an end whose pool rises above the pipe's own level REVERSES and the network BACKS UP. That last is the reason to have done it: a fixed rate has no idea what is outside it and goes on pushing into a flood. A port is a facing with no pipe across it, so turning a facing into the run beside it CAPS that end, and `facingFor` will not point a new pipe into the run it has just joined — a capped end is a thing you may want and never the thing you want by default. Water moves between NEIGHBOURING cells rather than being shared across a network at once, both sides measured from the SILL between them, which is the hydrostatic reconstruction the solver does at every edge it has and is here for the same reason: what drives water from one cell into the next is the depth standing above the lip it has to cross. Done as one level for the whole network — less code — it siphons, and the way it fails is worth recording: a nine cell run over a twenty half step ridge, fed at one end only, put 0.63 of water into every cell on the dry side, water that would have had to climb twenty half steps to get there. `totalVolume` now counts the columns, the air, the drips, what is standing in the pipes AND what is hanging at their mouths, because a total that stopped counting water the moment it entered a pipe would report a leak every time a drain worked; and since a pipe MAKES NO WATER the check is the strict form: not accounted for, but CONSTANT. It used to have a tap on it — a trickle per network, put there so a pipe you had just placed did something rather than sitting inert — and what that actually did was fill every pipe on every map out of nowhere, which is a worse thing to be wrong about than an inert stub is to look at. To feed a run, put water at one of its ports: a pour, a spring, a pool it reaches into. A pipe also has a LEVEL of its own, which is what lets it run UNDER things — see below.
- **And water in a pipe SLOSHES, because the edges carry MOMENTUM** (`world/water/pipe-flow.ts`). Levelling by relaxation gets the water to the right place and cannot overshoot, for the same reason anything without inertia cannot; momentum is the whole of the difference between a level that settles and a level that swings past and comes back. So each edge between two pipe cells carries a DISCHARGE, accelerated by the level difference across it times the area able to carry it, slowed by friction against the wall — which is the Saint-Venant equations in one dimension, and the same virtual-pipes scheme `fluid/columns` runs in two, except that here the pipes are real ones. Every trick the two dimensional solver needed is needed again and for the same reasons: both sides from the sill, outflows scaled to the water a cell actually has, friction quadratic and applied implicitly so it can slow a surge without ever reversing it, and a step worked out per step rather than assumed. Measured, a sealed eight tile run tilted and released swings 1.40 → 0.49 → 1.23 → 1.01 → 1.14 half steps at its closed end and comes back at 12.5 seconds against the 12.0 that `2 L / c` predicts for a closed channel — a number nothing in the code is told. A surge put in one end of a run reaches the far end later than the near end, and later still in a longer run, which is travel time and a relaxation has none of. **THE PIPE IS ROUND**, and that is not decoration: a circular bore is wide in the middle and narrow at the top, and the surface width is what turns a change in volume into a change in LEVEL, so the last of a pipe fills hard and the wave speed `sqrt(g A / T)` runs away as it does. Model it as a rectangle and a pipe fills placidly and surcharges with no warning. The area-to-depth relation of a circular segment has no closed form either way round, so it is tabulated once at 256 entries and read with a binary search, which is a handful of operations against a Newton solve per cell per substep. **And above the crown there is a SLOT** — Preissmann's: a full pipe has no free surface at all and is a pressure problem with an enormous wave speed, so it is given a hairline slot along the top and water rising past the crown is still a free surface, just a very narrow one. The level in the slot IS the pressure head, the equations never change form, and part full and surcharged are one code path rather than two with a switch between them; a fiftieth of the pipe's width puts the celerity up about sevenfold, which is a step this can afford, where a thousandth would be truer to real water hammer and cost thirty times the substeps to say so. A run under a pool deeper than it is tall now pressurises rather than refusing, and comes up to that pool's own surface, with `PIPE_HEAD` left as a rail on how far a port will push past the crown. The ports are stiff once surcharged for the same reason the slot is narrow — a very small volume is a very large change in level — so each takes its frame in six bites rather than one, which is the cheap version of solving it implicitly and brings a whole half step of error down to a seventh of one. It costs 37 microseconds a frame with a hundred cells of pipe on the map and 98 with four hundred, against 3.3 with none.

- **And you can SEE what is in a pipe** (`render/drips-gpu.ts`). A cell draws a LIMB towards each neighbour it is joined to and one out of whichever face it opens on, so the limbs of two neighbours meet at the face between them and a run you painted reads as a run; each limb is then drawn twice, once as casing and once as the water standing in it, with every casing of a cell going down before any of its water so that at a junction one limb's wall cannot be painted over the next limb's water. How full the bore is comes from `pipeDepth` — the same level a port measures its head from, so what is on the screen is the number that is driving the simulation and not a second opinion about it. The water lies along the BOTTOM of the bore, which sounds obvious and is the one thing that was wrong first time: a bar has two perpendiculars, they are the same line, and the one to settle the water against is whichever of them points down the screen rather than whichever the axis happened to produce. At the dozen pixels a pipe is thick there is no room to draw the curve of a free surface, so what it draws is HOW MUCH — an empty pipe is a line of casing, a half full one has a seam of water along its floor, a full one is water wall to wall, and a rim of casing is left showing all the way round so that even a full bore reads as water inside something. **Water under PRESSURE is lightened hard**, which is the only thing on the map that can tell you at a glance which parts of a network are surcharged and which are merely full; it is a tint row rather than a shader branch, so it costs nothing. The drawn bore is deliberately slimmer than the simulated one: `PIPE_D` is two half steps, which against this projection's foreshortening is half a tile across, and a pipe drawn at its true size is a tunnel lying on the ground.
- **A pipe has its own INVERT, so it can run under a hill** (`pipeZ` in `world/grid.ts`). Absolute, not a depth below the surface, and that is the whole of it: a depth below the surface FOLLOWS the surface, so a run crossing a ridge climbs the ridge and is stopped by it exactly as a surface pipe was, while an invert of its own keeps its grade whatever the ground does over the top. There is no "buried" flag and there does not need to be one — a pipe is buried when the ground beside it happens to be higher than its invert, which is a question you ask at the moment you need the answer, and it means raising ground over a pipe buries it and digging out from under one leaves it exposed, both of which are what actually happens. **Nothing chooses the depth**, either: `layPipe` takes the lower of the ground it is on and the run it is joining, so a run lies on the ground while the ground behaves, burrows under anything that rises in front of it, and comes back up to daylight wherever the ground falls away. One rule, no setting, and it is the only version of this that survives being drawn with a mouse. The other half is that **a buried opening is SEALED**: a hole in the side of a pipe under a hillside opens onto rock, and rock is not something to draw from or discharge into, so a port whose mouth sits below the ground it opens onto reads exactly like a capped end. That is what makes a buried run a closed conduit whose openings are its inlet and its outfall and nothing in between — without it a run would empty into the hill it was crossing. What you get is a CULVERT, or an inverted siphon, and it is worth naming which: the pipe goes under the obstacle and the head behind pushes its water up the far side. A true siphon goes OVER and holds itself up by suction, and this scheme cannot do one and does not pretend to, because a free surface cannot be at less than nothing. A buried run is drawn as a GHOST of itself, fading with depth — it cannot vanish, since a run you cannot see is a run you cannot edit, and it cannot stay solid, since it would read as lying on the grass it is in fact under. The `culvert` fixture is two pockets in solid rock with a run buried under the ridge between them: over the ground they are separate worlds and the solver is right about that, and the right-hand pocket has no tap, so anything that appears in it came through the pipe.
- **X-RAY: see through the ground to what is buried in it** (`y`, or the `xray` overlay). Not drawn like the other overlays, because there is nothing to draw — the pipework and the water standing in it are already on the screen every frame, above the ground tiers, and the only thing in the way is the hill. So this takes the hill down to a quarter and leaves everything else exactly as it was, which means a buried run needs no second renderer and cannot disagree with the one that draws it the rest of the time. Alpha on the three ground TIERS per band, not on four thousand sprites, so it is three assignments a band and only on a change; the cliff columns go translucent with the tiles above them, which is what makes a hillside something you can see into rather than a pane of glass laid over it. With it on, a buried run stops being drawn as a ghost and is drawn like any other — a faint pipe seen through a hill you can already see into is just hard to read.
- **THE GROUND A NEW COMPANY FOUNDS ON IS CARVED, NOT ROUTED** (`world/gen/generate-map.ts`). Routing a road over existing terrain means pathfinding for a route flat enough to build beside, and that search can fail; carving cannot. Terrain is generated first, the road is cut through it at ONE height, and every cell is then clamped to within **a slab a tile** of the street — a cone, not a shoulder. The difference matters: a shoulder blends the land towards the road by a FRACTION of the drop, so it keeps frontage gentle only until somebody raises the relief, while the cone is absolute and simply stops binding once it is wider than the hills. The player is guaranteed a flat street with buildable frontage on both sides, whatever the noise did.
- **The first heightfield read as static, not landscape** (`world/gen/generate-map.ts`). Four octaves of noise taken straight: **75% of cells sat a single half step from a neighbour** — a 16.5px ledge on three cells in four, no plane anywhere big enough to build on, and every house needing its ground levelled first. Three things fixed it, and each is a measured 40-seed number. The fourth octave's wavelength is under two tiles, so all it could express was a one-cell bump — dropped. The land is **quantised to whole slabs**, which is the step the tileset is drawn for (a ground frame is a diamond plus a 33px skirt, and that skirt IS one step of wall), so every cliff on a generated map lines up with its art instead of being faked on a half step. And the signed height is raised to a power, which pulls noise's crowded middle towards nought and leaves the extremes alone, so the map spends its relief on a few real hills instead of on general unevenness. **Level neighbourhoods went 21.8% → 64%, and the drop histogram from 75% half-step ledges to nothing but whole slabs**, while relief went UP: mean 15 → 30 half steps, because the field is also stretched to its own extremes first (several octaves summed span maybe a third of nought-to-one, so a map given sixteen half steps of relief was using five).
- **What the ground is MADE of is a rule the player can act on** (`paintGround`). Four, in precedence order: **the verge is earth** — and the verge is exactly the set of cells housing may be built on, so the art gives the placement rule away rather than making a player hunt for legal frontage by clicking; **the brow of a two-slab cliff is earth**, because the face below it is drawn in earth already and grass over it reads as a lawn on a quarry; **the high ground is earth and the low ground is sand**, the pair that makes elevation readable without counting slabs — and the sand is a promise about drainage, marking where water will collect once there are rivers. Both altitude lines are displaced by their own slow noise, or they would be **contours**: smooth closed curves at exactly one height, which no coastline is. All three materials fall back to the grass index, so a caller with one tile still gets a map.
- **THE MAP HAS TWO INPUTS, AND KEEPING THEM APART IS THE WHOLE POINT** (`world/gen/params.ts`). The seed picks WHICH map — where the hills are, where the street wanders, where the water lies. The eighteen parameters pick what KIND: how tall, how flat, how wet, how wooded. Holding the seed and moving one slider therefore gives the same country under different weather, which is the only way to tune terrain generation by eye — change both at once and you cannot tell what your change did. That is a constraint on the code and not just a description of it: every random draw comes from the seed alone and every parameter enters as a magnitude, never as another source of randomness, and a test holds it by generating the same seed at both ends of the land settings and asserting the street has not moved. **The slider table IS the UI**: `GEN_SLIDERS` carries each knob's range, label and explanation, the editor's panel is generated from it, and a test asserts the table and the parameter type have exactly the same keys — so a new knob is one entry and appears correctly bounded, rather than a panel that goes stale the first time somebody forgets. The ranges are the range the GUARANTEES survive, not the range the arithmetic survives, and a test walks every slider to both ends and asks for a flat street, one connected road and buildable frontage back.
- **THE STREET WANDERS, AND EVERYTHING IS MEASURED FROM IT BY BREADTH-FIRST SEARCH** (`world/gen/road.ts`). The first draft ran two dead-straight rows across the middle of every map, which made the seed a decoration — you could tell two maps apart by their hills and by nothing else. The spine is a slow noise curve along whichever axis the seed chose, and the autotiler already had the corner, T and dead-end frames to draw it. What made that affordable is replacing "which row is the road on" with a **distance field**: one BFS from every paved cell, after which the flat carve, the frontage count and the material rules all work for a street of any shape without knowing what shape it is. A curve that moves more than a tile of cross axis per tile of long axis would leave the road in disconnected rungs — invisible on a 64² map and fatal to the road graph, since "is this house on a street" is asked of it — so the gap is filled, and the test that catches it counts **components**, not paved cells. It only fires at the far end of the wander slider, which is exactly why the slider-range test exists.
- **THE CARVE IS A CONE, NOT A SHOULDER** (`world/gen/generate-map.ts`). Ground is clamped to within a slab a tile of the street, everywhere, which stops binding by itself once it is wider than the hills. A shoulder — blend the land towards the road over a fixed few tiles — keeps frontage gentle only until somebody raises the relief, because the blend is a FRACTION of the drop; a cap on the climb per tile is absolute. No seam either, because the constraint fades out instead of stopping.
- **A RIVER RUNS ONTO THE MAP AND OFF IT AGAIN, and it is FLOW that keeps it full** (`world/gen/water.ts`). A course is picked between two cells on the map's boundary that are reachable from one another — which is how "the same side of the street" is expressed without anyone working out where the street is, since the road's clearance splits the map in two. The ends are chosen LOW on purpose: the whole course is held near one waterline, so every tile of land above it has to be cut away, and picking high ends quarried **1,164 cells of a 4,096-cell map to a depth of 32 half steps**. Low ends keep the cut a valley — and they are also why a dam cannot hold the water in, because the ends are then the lowest cells on the course. That is not a problem to be solved but the right physics: the river is full because water keeps arriving, not because something is holding it.
- **THE RIVER IS PLANNED BEFORE THERE IS ANY LAND, and the land is built around it** (`world/gen/valley.ts`). This is the inversion the whole generator turns on. The country used to be shaped out of noise and a channel cut through whatever it made, which is the wrong way round and measurably so: over eight seeds the finished bed **CLIMBED 38 half steps along its own course against a net fall of 15**, and on the worst of them 100 against 14. A bed that goes back uphill is not a river, it is a row of basins, and no amount of water makes it flow. It could not have come out otherwise — `relief` is 16 half steps and the river's entire fall was 14, so the noise was larger than the gradient it was being asked to run down, and nothing in the pipeline ever made the bed DESCEND. Now the course is chosen first, geometrically, and the ground is a function of it: a floor that falls from inlet to mouth, land climbing away from the spine at a slab a tile, and the fbm laid on top as detail that fades out as it nears the water. The bed descends because it was never allowed to do anything else — **the planned floor climbs by exactly nothing, on every seed**, and a test asserts it rather than measuring a map.
- **What puts the climb back is the STREET, and that is a repair rather than the mechanism.** The land is clamped into a cone around the road, so where a river passes close the cone lifts its floor towards the road's level: on one seed it lifted 115 of 235 spine cells by as much as 20.9 half steps and put 50 half steps of climb back into a bed that had none. So the carve keeps a running minimum of the floor along the course. That looks like the old code and is not the same thing at all — there, a running minimum was the ONLY thing pushing the bed downhill and had to quarry a valley out of raw noise; here the floor is already right nearly everywhere and this bites in the few places the street disagrees. Final numbers over eight seeds: grade **0.058 → 0.198** half steps a cell, climb **38 → 11**, every course reaching the rim at both ends.
- **And it has to CROSS the map, not run round the outside of it.** Two edge cells can be a long way apart and still both sit near one corner — (1,63) and (0,0) are sixty-four apart on a 64² map and the line between them *is* the left-hand edge. A course laid there is a course laid on the absorbing boundary, leaking off the side of the world down its whole length: that seed's river held **19% of its channel where one crossing the middle held all of it**. So the chord's midpoint has to be properly inland, and the walk carries a penalty for hugging the rim until it is time to leave by it. Removing either one is caught by the end-to-end test below.
- **The generator's SETTINGS outlive the map they made** (`world/io/gen-settings.ts`). Two different things kept two different ways: the map is what you have, the sliders are the terms the next one would be made on, and turning up the relief, generating a dozen times and then refreshing should not put the relief back. Written straight through with no debounce, which is the opposite of what the map next door does and for the reason given there — `world-save` is avoiding tens of kilobytes of base64 per painted cell, and this is a couple of dozen numbers, so writing it on every frame of a slider drag costs nothing and spares the whole question of flushing on the way out. Read back through `withDefaults`, which is not politeness about old files: a localStorage key is a text box anybody can type into, so keys added since take their defaults, every value is clamped to its own slider's range, and anything that is not a finite number is dropped — including, as a test found, the indices of an array, which `typeof` calls an object and which spread straight into the settings. **Reset FORGETS** rather than writing the current defaults, so a default that changes in the code still reaches somebody who asked for the defaults.
- **A VALLEY COSTS LEVEL GROUND, and the only lever is its FOOTPRINT.** Measured across a grid of widths and depths: three tiles wide to fourteen takes the map from 53% level to 41%, while flat to twelve half steps deep costs **one point**. Shape is nearly free and area is not, so the valley is narrow where the river arrives and broadens as it deepens, at a side grade of one slab a tile — the grade the cliff art is drawn for. Two things were tried and measured worse and are worth not trying again: fading the NOISE in over the whole valley side rather than the shape (no measurable difference — the ramp was never the cost), and tilting the whole map so the valley could keep one depth (**51% → 41%**, because `along` is the nearest SPINE CELL's position, which is a sensible number near the water and a patchwork far from it, jumping across the middle between two loops of a meander — as a tilt that is not a plane, it is a set of terraces facing different ways).
- **A river is FULL when the map opens, and stays full** (`carveChannel`, `standWater`). The channel is filled to the valley floor it was cut below — the level the flow will hold it at, which the carve knows because it drew it. Only where there is something to hold it: unfed, a course open at both ends drains, and filling it leaves water standing ten half steps over its own bank for the solver to pour away. Measured over eight seeds, the spine is **98% wet at spawn and 95% a simulated minute later**, against 64% and 66% before. The median cell on the shore has a freeboard of exactly **nought** — the water is level with the lowest ground touching it — with a tenth to a fifth of the shore briefly over its bank at the moment the map opens and all of it back inside within half a minute.
- **AND IT IS NOT SETTLED, which is the mistake worth recording.** Relaxing the primed level until every cell sits below its lowest way out is the standard companion to a priority flood, and it is exactly wrong here: it computes the NO-FLOW equilibrium, which is what the flood already returned, so it drained the channel straight back to 8% and undid the whole point. A flowing surface stands above the static one in places — that is what flow means. The overshoot is about a slab, four at its worst where two arms of a meander run either side of a ridge, and the solver trims it inside half a minute. The tests say both halves: standing water is **never** over its bank, and a fed one is back inside it within thirty seconds.
- **A POINT SOURCE CANNOT FEED A RIVER** (`feed`). `Grid.source` is an `Int8Array`, so one cell tops out at 127 half steps a second however hard it is pushed — and at any rate a single cell leaves a channel that drains faster than it fills. Measured on a course that had been running dry: one cell wet **none of it**; the same rate laid ACROSS the channel's own width took it to **56% of its length with three cells of spill** anywhere else on the map. The other direction is just as sharp — at 127 over a three-tile radius the volume converges beautifully and **3,734 of 4,096 tiles end up under water**, which is a flood, not a river. The rate is the correctness matter here and it fails quietly in the low direction, so a test runs the solver and compares the same map fed and unfed.
- **THE WATER IS PLACED BY A PRIORITY FLOOD, not cell by cell while carving** (`fillDepressions`). The level is not a property of a cell; it is a property of the BASIN the cell is in, and nothing local can know it. Filling to a fixed depth over each cell's own bed meant a course that descends — every course — got water at a level the cell downstream was already below, and the solver moved it all to the bottom. The flood starts from every cell that drains, always takes the lowest frontier next, and so reaches everything over the lowest rim there is: a basin comes out filled exactly to its spill point and a slope comes out dry. The map therefore **opens at equilibrium** instead of spending seconds settling. The street is seeded as an outlet rather than clipped afterwards, which is what keeps the frontage dry HONESTLY — clip the answer and the solver flows into it a second later.
- **THE FIRST RIVERS WERE PUDDLES, and the measurement is what said so** (`world/gen/water.ts`). Twenty-five wet cells in a bounding box of four by five, one depth the whole way, one width. The cause was a source chosen for HEIGHT alone: the carve clamps the land into a cone around the street, so the highest ground on every map is as far from the street as it can get, which is hard against an edge — the sources were three tiles from their own outlets. Inland is a requirement now, not a preference, and height only chooses between the cells that already have a river's worth of run in them. Mean spine length went **from about fifteen cells to a hundred and thirty**.
- **WALKED FIRST, CARVED AFTERWARDS**, which is the change the rest depends on. A river's width, its depth and the fall of its bed are all functions of how far along it you are — and you cannot know that until you know how long it is, which is why cutting as it went gave one width and one depth from source to sea. Now the course is a path, and then: the bed follows a **concave** long profile, because that is the shape every river on earth has and a straight line is one uniform ramp — half the drop happens inside the first third. The channel **tapers**, one tile wide at the head to `riverWidth` at the mouth and a third of `riverDepth` to all of it, by a square root, which is roughly how discharge and width actually relate. And the valley **flares with its own depth**: each slab of cut buys `riverBank` tiles of bank, so a shallow reach has a lip and a gorge has a side to it, rather than every cut ending in the sheer face a fixed bank width leaves behind.
- **THE WALK HAS TWO PHASES, because following the land and arriving somewhere are different jobs.** While there is budget it reads the ground — downhill, with enough noise across the slope to meander, which on terraced land is what stops it being a staircase of straight runs. When the budget is gone it makes for the outlet, every step strictly decreasing the distance to it. That distance is a **breadth-first field over cuttable ground only**: the street spans the map and channels keep clear of it, so the cells a river may cut fall into two regions with no way between them, and one BFS from every edge cell gives each side its own distance to its own three outlets without anyone working out which side they are on. It makes arrival a proof rather than a hope, and it replaced "head for the nearer map edge", which is only the same thing when the street runs down the middle.
- **THREE BUGS THAT ONLY A MEANDER CAN HAVE.** A course that wanders comes back alongside itself, and every one of these is that fact meeting code that assumed it would not. **The bed ratcheted**: measured against the LIVE ground, a channel reads what it has already cut and takes another river's depth off it — a default map came out with a chasm to the floor of the world, **height −126**, from twenty crossings at six half steps each. Every bed is measured against a copy of the land as it was. **The waterline stacked**: an upstream reach's level sat over a downstream reach's much lower bed and filled the gorge with fifty half steps of standing water; a channel now holds a channel's depth and no more. **And the banks cut the road**: the spine keeps its distance from the street but the valley stamped around it is wider than the spine, so a course running past at the clearance limit took its banks through the road — nothing threw, the paving stayed, the ground under it dropped, and the road graph came apart into **fifteen pieces**. The carve is clipped to what may be cut, and the test that caught it counts road components.
- **A SIDE STREAM THAT DID NOT REACH ITS RIVER IS NOT A SIDE STREAM.** Tributaries are walked with the same walker towards a distance field measured to the river instead of to the sea. One that gets boxed in and is carved anyway is an orphan watercourse starting nowhere and ending nowhere — three maps in fifteen had their water in two or three disconnected pieces. Carved only on arrival, and a test asserts a generated map's water is **one connected system**.
- **THE MAP IS FED OVER ITS OWN EDGE, not by a spring standing near one** (`inflowAt`, and @see `Grid.inflow` above). A spring is a RATE, and the right rate is a function of each channel's grade and section — measured across one fixture's geometries it ran from nine to eighty half steps a second a cell, so one number is a trickle on some maps and a marsh on others. Worse, a river has to reach the map's edge to have an outlet, so the spring sat ON the boundary, where the open edge empties the outer ring every substep: a tap there keeps about **a sixth** of what the same tap three tiles in keeps. The old code fought this with a BAR across each end — a dam on the map's own rim — and with a weir at every step of the waterline, which is what made a river read as a chain of ponds and put 100, 10 and 88 half steps of climb back into beds whose whole net fall was 14 to 16. All of that is gone. The inlet holds the rim at the river's own surface across its whole cross-section and delivers whatever discharge that level drives; the mouth is the ordinary absorbing edge, and the river pours off the side of the world because that is what an outlet is.
- **A RIVER NEVER CROSSES THE STREET, and that is structural rather than checked.** There is no bridge in the tileset, so a river that crossed would either dam itself against the road or wash over it, and both break the promise that a player can build along the whole street. The head picks the edge on its own side and walks straight at it — a path whose cross coordinate moves one tile towards its edge every step cannot come back round — and every channel keeps two tiles of clearance from any paved cell, which is also what stops it eating the frontage the cone just guaranteed.
- **The `pipes` fixture is the whole chain, running itself** (`world/debug/fixtures.ts`). A spring feeds a sloping walled CHANNEL; a run of pipe lying along it draws at its uphill end and discharges over the drop at its downhill one, in drops, into a small walled pocket; a branch off the middle is turned back into itself and so is capped, and fills until it is standing above its own crown under pressure. The pocket is deliberately small, so inside a minute it comes up past the spout, the head reverses, the dripping stops and the whole run backs up and tracks the pool it was feeding. Four behaviours in order, with nothing to set up, and a test walks the same minute and asserts them in that order — which makes it the only end-to-end check there is of the chain from a tap on a map through a network to a drop in the air and back again. The channel carries water to the same drop the pipe does, and that is not a compromise: a pipe lying ON the ground cannot be the only way out of anything, because whatever it crosses to get out, water can cross too. The first draft was a wide shelf behind a weir, and it was right and useless — the pond needed five minutes to reach the weir, and a fixture nobody will sit through is a fixture nobody will run.
- **Springs and drains are a RATE, and they are a layer.** A pour is a volume that arrives once, so what it makes runs out; a spring keeps arriving, so what it makes is a standing flow — a river is a source up high and a sink at the bottom, not a mode. One signed number per cell, positive in and negative out, because a drain is the same mechanism backwards; it is in half steps a second over the TILE, and resolution-independent because the same depth goes on every one of the tile's columns. Being an ordinary layer is what makes a spring undoable and saveable while the water it PRODUCES is neither, so a river reloads as a river rather than as the puddle it happened to be when you saved. A drain takes its rate only while water stands on it — it is a plughole, not a vacuum.
- **An OFF-MAP INFLOW is a LEVEL, not a rate** (`Grid.inflow`, `spill` in `fluid/columns.ts` and `fluid/gpu/spill.ts`). A spring delivers the same amount however full the channel already is, so somebody has to choose a number that fills the river without drowning the country round it — and that number is a function of the channel's own grade and section. Measured across one fixture's geometries the right rate ran from **nine to eighty half steps a second a cell**: there is no one value, so a rate-fed river is a trickle or a marsh depending on the map it landed on. An inflow instead holds the map's rim at a water SURFACE and delivers whatever discharge that surface drives — much at first, less as the channel backs up, nothing once it is full to the line. The flow tunes itself to the channel, and **overfilling is not representable** rather than merely avoided, because water cannot stand above a line it is being held at. Measured on the `inlet` fixture, which is built on the slack grade and the deep channel generated maps actually have: fed by a spring it fills **a third** of its channel in the minute an inflow fills **all** of it, and in eight simulated minutes of holding the level two half steps PROUD of the channel's own brim, not one tile of bank ever got wet.
- **And it belongs to the BOUNDARY, not to a controller** — which is why it is in `spill` beside the open edge rather than in `runSources` beside the springs. A stage held by pouring the difference has to READ the depth to know the difference, and on the device path the host's copy is three to five frames behind: that is a feedback loop with dead time, and it rings. A boundary condition has no history to be stale — `depth = max(0, level - ground)` is the same answer whoever asks and whenever, so it runs on the device every substep against the device's own state. An absorbing edge is the same expression with the level below the ground, so there is no branch a map without an inflow takes and a map with one does not. It is the one rule in the solver **written twice on purpose**, once per path — including the walk round the perimeter, which each side spells out for itself because a compute pass indexes it by thread and the host by loop. That is a duplicated rule and it is held by two things rather than by hope: the host's `rimAt` is pinned to the property (it names every border column and no interior one), and `?gpucheck` diffs the two solvers pass by pass — every pass agrees to about 1e-7, which is where the untouched fixtures sit too. The one thing that is not obvious: `spill` writes depth directly rather than through `addWater`, so it owes the active box and the deepest column by hand. Without the box the solver looks at an empty region and does nothing at all, which presents as a rim held perfectly at its level and a map still bone dry half a minute later.
- **The `inlet` fixture is the hard case on purpose** (`world/debug/fixtures.ts`, and it is what the debug panel's `river` button now loads). The old `river` fixture is a straight lane on a grade steep enough that any feed runs down it, so it passes whatever the boundary does — it proved the solver could carry a river long before the maps could hold one. This one is built out of the four things that were actually wrong with generated maps, measured on them: a grade too slack to carry a spring (**0.1** against `river`'s 0.4, where generated courses fell 0.05 to 0.07), a channel cut deeper than a rate will ever fill (**6 half steps**, against the one to two a spring sustains), a feed ON the rim where the open edge empties the outer ring every substep (a tap there keeps about **a sixth** of what the same tap three tiles in keeps), and a course that bends, so nothing is a special case of a straight solve down one axis. **It opens FULL**, filled column by column at the level each column's own floodplain sits at — a waterline that descends with the valley, rather than the one flat level `pond` takes, which would drown the low end and leave the high end dry. Fed from dry the channel takes about a minute to reach its far edge, and for that minute the map shows something that is not what it is. Nothing needs to be exact about the fill: the inflow holds the head and the open edge takes the surplus, so the map converges on the same river whatever it starts with — this only decides whether you watch it happen. Its tests assert the channel is full **the moment the map opens**, that the water never gets out of the valley however hard it is fed, that it settles instead of filling the map — and that **the same ground fed by a spring loses most of it**, which is the one that makes the others mean something. That last is measured in VOLUME and not in wet tiles, and the difference is the whole of why: a tile counts as wet with half a half step on it, so a channel emptying under a film reads as full the entire way down. The spring keeps 97% of its tiles wet while losing nearly three quarters of its water — barely better than feeding it nothing at all.
- **A map may also hold water STANDING on it** (`Grid.pool`), which for a long time it could not, and the gap is worth naming because of what it cost. `Grid.fluid` recorded WHICH fluid had been poured on a cell and never how much — so a map could say a lake was here and never say how deep, every lake went down the drain on save, and a map opened as the empty basin it had been carved out of. It also meant every fixture with water in it had to be a SPRING: water arrived by being run in from somewhere and the map had to be watched while it filled, so anything about water at rest, or about what a waterfall does to a pool that is already there, could not be set up at all — only waited for, with the interesting part over by the time there was anything to look at. A DEPTH per cell, because a depth has a natural zero and a level does not: nought is dry wherever the ground is, while a level of nought is a real surface and "no water" would need a sentinel. Authoring is still in LEVELS, since a pond is a thing with a waterline — the fixture helper fills every cell in a box whose ground is under the line and leaves the rim alone, so a basin fills without anything having to say where the basin is. It is an INITIAL CONDITION and not a mirror: once the map is running the live depths are the truth and the layer does not follow them, the same way a source is a rate the world obeys rather than a record of what came out of it. **Saving is the one moment the map is told what water it has**, and that is not a contradiction — a save is a SNAPSHOT, and the two are different things. Pouring is not an edit: it puts water into the running world rather than into the grid, which is why it is not undoable and why the grid knows nothing about it, so a file written from the grid alone comes back as the empty basin the map started as however long you spent filling it. `serializeWorld` takes the live field and writes the water where it has actually got to, while the in-memory layer stays what it was authored as; re-opening the file makes that snapshot the new map's initial condition, which is exactly "save the world as it stands, open it as it was". What a snapshot cannot carry is said out loud rather than discovered: it is per tile and rounded to a half step, so a film thinner than half of one rounds away — friction always leaves one on a flat plain and a map that came back with a millimetre of water over every tile would be worse than one that came back dry — and what is in the AIR is not in it either, a fall in flight, a drop, what is standing in a pipe, which is a fraction of a second of the map's water and refills from the ports and springs that made it. Optional on read, like `ramp`, so a file written before this opens dry, which is what it was. The `plunge` fixture is what it buys — a river off a tall cliff into a pool that is already there, 2,121 wet tiles on the first frame — and it is the only way to look at a plunge pool without first waiting half a minute for the fall to build one.
- **The edge of the map is the edge of the world, not a wall.** The outermost ring of columns is emptied before every substep — an absorbing boundary, a depth held at zero — so water that reaches it has left. Without it a spring fills the world and the only way out is a hole you dug, which is fine for a reservoir and useless for a river. On by default and toggleable; the solver itself defaults to walled, because a test of how much water there is should not depend on where the water happened to be. It lets water off, it does not suck: on a flat plain friction still leaves a film, and only ground that slopes towards the edge drains dry. With it, a spring at the head of a valley and an open edge at its foot reach a standing flow — 262 units on the map at thirty seconds and 263 at ninety, with sixteen a second going in and the same going off the side. That is a river, and it is a source, a slope and a boundary rather than a feature.
- **Build cursor.** All placement routes through `world/edit/cursor.ts`: the tool supplies cells, the cursor draws them. The footprint outline lives in the overlay *above* the world so it stays readable, while ghost sprites are parented into their own cells' bands so they are occluded at true depth. Cells are tinted **individually**, so a partly blocked footprint shows which cells are the problem. It redraws on change (a signature over cells, heights, validity, frame and scale), not per frame.

## Dev tools

- A **building-kit labeller** (dev-only, `dev/roadlabel.html`) authors `building-kits.json` / `road-labels.json` / `slope-labels.json` for the renderers; its Slopes tab labels form, rise (×0.5 / ×1 only), direction, per-edge state and tunnels. ⚠️ These files hold hand-authored data — never overwrite them programmatically (e.g. via preview eval).
- The **world editor** at `?world=1` is the authoring surface for World v2: paint any of ~930 atlas frames, raise/lower/flatten/smooth terrain, place and demolish structures, brush shapes and sizes (a **road** line drag bends once into an L, since cells touching only at a corner are not adjacent here and would lay lone squares rather than a road), undo/redo, save/load, resize, pave and unpave roads (ramps appear on their own where a road crosses a step), overlays for the grid, bands, height, iso origin, road mask and network components, a per-cell inspector that reports which autotile rule fired, and **fixtures** that build a whole map on demand. The terrain ones (ziggurat / occluder / ramp fan / road shapes / avenue / plaza / split trap) make a picking or connectivity bug reproducible; the WATER ones (river / cascade / lake / islands / pipes / culvert / plunge) arrive with their own taps in the `source` layer and, since `Grid.pool`, with water already STANDING on them, so loading one starts a flow rather than handing you a shape to pour into — and `lake` is a lake on the first frame rather than a hole that becomes one in half a minute, while `plunge` puts a river off a tall cliff into a pool that is already there, which is the only way to watch a plunge pool without first waiting for the fall to build one — a graded valley reaching a standing flow, terraces each holding a pool at its lip, a basin fed from a shelf and drained through a notch, and blocks at one, two, four and eight steps each pouring off its own edges into water that is already there, which is the bed for checking what water on a raised tile looks like. In dev it also exposes the store as `window.__world` — a console `await import()` of the store resolves through a *different* Vite module graph and returns a second, unrelated instance, so that handle is the only reliable way to inspect the live map from outside React.
- **A COPY IS NOT A COMPOSITE, and the difference is a halo** (`dev/png-min.ts`). `blit` skipped fully transparent source pixels and copied everything else straight, which is right only when a sprite has no soft edge. The tileset is antialiased: the tree prop carries **208 partly transparent pixels** around its outline. Copied rather than composited, each one REPLACES an opaque pixel of the tile underneath with a half-transparent one — not a soft edge but a hole, with the grass that was there gone and whatever is behind the tile showing through. Stamped a few hundred times across a map it reads as a ring of light around every tree. Source-over with straight (non-premultiplied) alpha fixes it: **904 holes healed** in the tree sheet, and the 1,873 partly transparent pixels left are the grass tile's own silhouette, which should stay. The road-corner baker shares the function and had the same fault more quietly — 138 pixels, at the edges of the kerb nubs, invisible until you difference the two sheets.
- **`bun run bake:trees`** regenerates `isometric_assets/derived/trees_sheet.{png,xml}`: the grass tile with one, two and three trees stamped on it, from `cityDetails_010.png` (the same free-standing prop v1 scatters on unbuilt plots). A wood is therefore a terrain MATERIAL rather than a structure standing on one, which is the whole point — it saves, paints, undoes and renders through the paths that already exist, and the map does not grow a second kind of object with a footprint and placement rules for scenery nobody interacts with. The trade is that you cannot fell one tree; you repaint the cell. Trees sit low on the tile because a ground frame has no headroom — 132×99 is a 66px diamond and a 33px skirt with the diamond's top vertex at y=0, and a tree is 45px tall.
- **`bun run bake:road-corners`** regenerates `isometric_assets/derived/roadCorners_sheet.{png,xml}` from the landscape sheet. Derived output, checked in; re-run it if the road labelling or the notch rects change. `dev/png-min.ts` is a ~120-line PNG codec used only by the bakers — the project ships no image library and this avoids adding one for a build script.
- Some dev affordances (e.g. "grant juice") are gated to `localhost` via `utils/dev-mode.ts`.

## Testing

`bun test` runs unit/integration tests colocated as `*.test.ts`, plus headless progression simulations under `src/simulation`. App tsconfig excludes test files, so run them through Bun (not `tsc`).
