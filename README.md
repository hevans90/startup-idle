# Startup Idle

A satirical incremental/idle game about scaling a tech startup by any means necessary — hire interns, unleash vibe coders, anoint 10x devs, and watch an isometric city grow as your headcount does. Built as a single-page React app with a PixiJS-rendered city.

## Gameplay

You found a startup as one of several **founder archetypes**, each bending a different progression curve, then grow the company through interlocking systems:

- **Employees (generators)** — three roles (`intern`, `vibe_coder`, `10x_dev`) that produce money and innovation each tick. Hire more and buy **upgrades** to scale output.
- **Innovation** — a soft global multiplier (`1 + logMult · log₁₀(innovation+1)`) on all output, plus the currency for **managers** and unlocks.
- **Managers** — auto-tiering multipliers for money / innovation / valuation; assign them and they progress on their own.
- **Valuation & board mandates** — valuation accrues from revenue; spend it on permanent global mandates.
- **Employee management & satisfaction** — perks (money/innovation/cost/auto-buy) and a morale system that drifts each tick and feeds back into output.
- **Team leaders** — one named employee per role (`intern`, `vibe_coder`, `10x_dev`). Hire from a pool of 3 candidates, each with a random **trait** (e.g. `overachiever`, `chaos_agent`, `brown_noser`) that sets their output/morale bias and unlocks a 4-skill pool. Employees accumulate `outputStat` and `moraleStat` over time, gaining **ranks** that award skill points to spend on up to 3 skills (max level 3 each) — revenue multipliers, IPS boosts, satisfaction offsets, rank-compounding effects, and more. Skills have cross-role effects: `chaos_spark` debuffs *other* roles' satisfaction; `team_morale` buffs *all* roles. Certain trait pairings unlock **pair synergies** (e.g. `productive_chaos`, `dumpster_fire`) with additional output-growth or satisfaction penalties. Firing an employee costs innovation per rank and leaves a permanent **legacy bonus** (rev + IPS) for that role.
- **Vape shop** — 53 achievements across all game systems earn *vape juice*; spend it on 12 impactful upgrades (money output, innovation, valuation rate, hire cost reduction, equity payout) that visibly modify a high-def SVG vape rendered as a compact overlay on the map canvas (bottom-left). Achievements and upgrades persist through prestige resets. Version `0.2.0` bumped the upgrade roster; returning players' vape progress resets on load.
- **Slop pit** — a toxic sludge pool (`state/slop-pit.store.ts`) that unlocks at 50 vibe coders and slowly fills based on headcount. Occupies a reserved 5×5 plot in the vibe_coder district (world-gen skips building placement there). Above 50% fill it applies an income penalty (up to −85% at 100%). Draining costs −40 vibe_coder satisfaction. Renders in the isometric city with a fixed 5×5 pit footprint (camera-facing walls, dark floor), sludge that rises vertically from the pit bottom to ground level as fill increases, and a real-time height-field wave simulation (`office/slop-pit-fluid.ts`) that propagates ripple rings across the 5×5 surface grid. Drips from animated pipe fittings land on the sludge and inject splash impulses into the wave field; bubble rings float at the live sludge surface level. A DRAIN button empties the pit.
- **AI singularity** — an ominous meter that creeps up when vibe coders are miserable.
- **Founders** — six archetypes (Hacker, Bootstrapper, Visionary, Hustler, Agentic Delusionist, NEET) each with a unique *per-founder scaling modifier* that grows stronger with every acquisition made as that specific founder (tracked in `state/exits.store.ts`, persisted). Dynamic perk text shows current bonus levels. NEET starts with $5 and no base bonuses but doubles all money output with every exit (×2^n). Chosen on a full-screen select before the game begins; cards show the exit count badge and a scaling-modifier description.
- **Offline progression** — on return, the real game tick is replayed in chunks over the time away (full credit, capped at 2 days), with a "while you were away" popup.
- **Company Acquisition (prestige)** — once you've built enough *total accrued valuation*, accept an acquisition offer to bank permanent **Equity** (diminishing-returns payout), soft-reset into a fresh company (re-picking a founder; Equity, skill tree and board mandates persist), and spend Equity in a Path-of-Exile-style **skill tree** (~330 procedurally-laid-out nodes across themed clusters with standalone keystones, rendered on its own PixiJS viewport — verified overlap-free, light/dark themed). Costs escalate per node allocated; hovering previews the cheapest route (with total cost) and a click allocates the whole route at once; the tree is **respeccable** (Exits buy persistent respec points; one click refunds a node and its dependents). Includes fuzzy node **search** and hover-to-spotlight from the allocated-bonuses panel. The full loop — accrual tracking, payout, soft reset, allocation, respec, persistence — is in, and node **effects are wired to the economy**: every allocated grant resolves (`resolvePrestigeModifiers`) into multipliers applied at the same chokepoints as the founder modifiers (money, innovation, valuation, employee output, headcount synergy, automation, manager speed, hire cost, singularity, satisfaction-gain rate, Equity payout). **All keystones are fully implemented**, including their structural reshapes — Bootstrapped disables managers & auto-buy, Crunch Mode switches satisfaction off, Enshittify halves positive satisfaction bonuses, AGI-Pilled cripples interns, Permanent Acqui-hire grants free starting intern levels — each surfaced in the relevant UI (see `docs/acquisition-skill-tree-design.md`).
- **Isometric city** — a PixiJS + pixi-viewport scene that visualizes each district's headcount as growing building stacks, with hover highlighting and HTML info popovers.

## Architecture notes

- **State & persistence.** Each system is a Zustand store under `src/state`, persisted to `localStorage` via the `persist` middleware. Large numbers are `break_infinity.js` `Decimal`s serialized via `decimalReplacer`/`decimalReviver` (see `state/_break_infinity.decimals.ts`); store `merge` functions use `coerceDecimal` to defensively re-hydrate any legacy/foreign shape so a malformed save can't crash on first render.
- **Version wipe.** `state/version.store.ts` holds `CURRENT_VERSION`; bumping the major or minor triggers a full progress wipe (storage + in-memory) on next load via `useCompareVersion`. Use this for breaking changes.
- **The game loop** runs in `App.tsx` (a `setInterval`), ticking generators and managers. Stores expose per-second getters (`getMoneyPerSecond`, `getInnovationPerSecond`, `getValuationPerSecond`) that mirror the tick's multiplier chains, so displayed rates match actual earnings.
- **Isometric renderer.** Buildings are derived deterministically from headcount (`office/city/compute-city.ts`) and drawn into depth-sorted Pixi containers (`cityDepthKey`, column-dominant). Heights are monotonic in count to avoid "floor stealing."
- **World v2** (`src/world`, route `?world=1`) is a separate, mutable map engine that shares only the pure iso maths in `src/iso`; v1 under `src/office` is untouched. Terrain lives in dense typed arrays indexed `y*w+x` rather than keyed sets, so an edit updates one sprite per changed cell instead of rebuilding the map. Sprites are grouped into **diagonal bands** (one container per `x+y`): within a band sprites are a full tile apart at identical Y and cannot overlap, so nothing inside a band ever needs sorting. Elevation is in **half steps** (`HEIGHT_UNIT = 16.5`) and moves `wy` only — never `wx` — which is why band order and picking both stay cheap under verticality. Undo/redo is cell-index patches (`edit/commands.ts`), one command per drag stroke.
- **Verticality.** `height` is signed and measured in HALF steps (16.5px), because the tileset has exactly two rises — ×0.5 and ×1 — and nothing between; a full step is one slab skirt, so the field stays integral. Height moves `wy` only, never `wx`, which is why elevation costs the renderer nothing. A cell taller than its visible neighbours grows a **column**: copies of its own tile stacked at `HEIGHT_STEP`, each one's top hidden by the copy above, so only the skirts show — the tileset's skirt already shades its two faces differently, so nothing is tinted by hand. Columns live in their own per-band tier beneath the tiles, the one place ordering within a band matters.
- **Picking under elevation.** A screen point is ambiguous once the ground is not flat, but only along ONE diagonal, because `wx` fixes `x − y` whatever the height. `pickCell` marches that diagonal front to back and takes the first cell whose top face contains the point; there is exactly one candidate per band, since `x − y` must be an integer within 1 of the point's own and the two options have opposite parity. `faceCoords` solves the face exactly, so a **ramp's tilted parallelogram** works the same as a flat diamond. Tested against a brute-force scan rather than against the previous implementation, whose answers at shared face edges came out of rounding.
- **Ramps are DERIVED from paving and height**, not placed by hand. A road crossing a step needs a ramp to be traversable, so the ramp is what makes the road a road rather than a separate decision: pave between two heights and it appears, erase the road or level the ground and it goes. The ramp always sits on the LOWER cell rising toward its higher neighbour, so a staircase ramps continuously. `roads/ramp-derive.ts` refuses what the artset cannot draw — a half step (no paved ×0.5 art), a step of two or more, or a cell facing two higher neighbours — and the road simply stays split, which the network overlay shows. Ramp frames differ in height by direction: N and E rise up-screen and are 131px tall, while S and W rise toward the viewer and fit the raise inside the 99px a flat tile already has, which is why a slope sprite is anchored by the standard ground height rather than its own frame. The whole derivation happens inside the paving command (via `PatchBuilder.peek`, which reads the staged edit rather than the grid), so one undo reverses road and ramp together. `ramp-art.ts` reads the hand-authored `slope-labels.json` and filters it to walkable ground: no water (most gently tilted art is river and shoreline), no road-extent embankments, no corner rises.
- **Roads are a paved AREA, not a line.** Forced by the artwork: the tileset has no thin outer corner, so a line model cannot draw a bend. You paint cells and the region autotiles, which also gets 2-wide avenues and plazas for free rather than special-casing them. The `mask → sprite` table is **derived** from `road-labels.json` / `city-tile-labels.json` at load, not hand-written — v1's hand-written table omits all four outer corners even though the art is labelled, so every v1 bend falls back to plain asphalt. `roadCoverage()` walks all 16 masks and reports which resolve exactly, which substituted and which have no art, because a silent fallback is what hides the failure — and the editor's `gaps` overlay marks the substituted cells on the map, so a labelling gap is visible rather than deduced. A turn picks its art from the DIAGONAL across its two open sides: where the paved region wraps round, the cell is a corner of a block and the road fills it (square-edged art); where it does not, a single-width road is turning, and the artset draws that only as a CURVE. Both are labelled, so the landscape set resolves every mask exactly; `roadCoverage` fails loudly if that ever regresses.
- **Derived road art is baked, not composited at runtime.** Where three paved cells meet a grass cell at a vertex the kerb has to turn 90° around that point, and no single tile carries a mixture of interior and notched corners. `bun run bake:road-corners` composites the 22 needed variants into `isometric_assets/derived/roadCorners_sheet.png`, which loads as a fourth atlas — so at runtime they are ordinary frames with the same nearest-neighbour sampling and the same batching as every other tile. Compositing at draw time was tried twice and fails twice over: separate sprites round to the pixel grid independently (a 1px drift at arbitrary zoom), and a `RenderTexture` defaults to linear filtering while the atlas is nearest (visibly soft). The variant list is derived from the autotile resolver, so the sheet cannot drift from what the engine asks for.
- **Dirty cells accumulate, and the renderer drains them.** Several commits inside one task collapse into a single React re-render, so a dirty set that each commit replaced left the renderer reconciling only the last one — every earlier edit in the grid and absent from the screen. It lives outside the store alongside `history` and the road graph, for the same in-place-mutation reason.
- **Structures are a definition plus a render STRATEGY** (`src/world/structures`). A `StructureDef` is data — a footprint, a `RenderSpec`, and rules for where it may stand — and nothing in `def.ts` or `place.ts` imports Pixi, so the placement rules are testable without a renderer. Drawing goes through a registry keyed on `render.kind` (`tiles`, `excavation`, `custom`), each strategy implementing `mount`/`update`/`unmount` against the band containers; an unregistered strategy resolves to null so one undrawable building never stops the world drawing. This is the abstraction v1 lacks: the slop pit IS a structure, but its footprint lives as hardcoded span constants inside `GroundRoadLayer`, so nothing can ask what occupies a cell or place a second one. `structureAt` stores a structure's **id**, never a list index — an index is not stable under demolish, nor under undo restoring records out of order. Placing is ONE command: level the footprint to its lower median, clear any ramp under it, stamp the footprint, create the record — so a single undo puts the hill back. `clearsTerrain` is a RENDER rule and not a data one; zeroing the terrain layer would lose the material, so demolishing could not restore it and every excavation would leave a permanent hole. The map file carries records as readable JSON and rebuilds `structureAt` by stamping them, which keeps 16KB per 64² map out of the file and means the layer cannot disagree with the records it indexes. Building stacks come from `composeBuilding` in `src/iso/kits.ts`, which v1 calls too — the rules for which frame sits on which floor are art facts and must not fork.
- **Water is a field of COLUMNS, simulated** (`src/fluid/columns.ts`, wired up in `src/world/water`, drawn by `render/water.ts`). Every cell holds a depth above whatever the ground is there, and the state is those depths plus the flux on the edges between them. A cell's surface is `ground + depth`, so water at any height flows to any other height — the only thing driving it is the difference between two surfaces, and **a waterfall is not a special case, it is a large head difference across one edge**. The scheme is virtual pipes (Mei, Decaudin & Neyret 2007): flux accelerated by the head TIMES the water able to carry it, slowed by drag, depths from the divergence. **Both sides of an edge are measured from the SILL between them**, not from the sea floor — hydrostatic reconstruction, after Audusse et al. 2004 — without which the scheme is only honest over gentle ground. Water standing on a plateau with water ten steps below it was driven by a head of ten, a pressure gradient no depth of water in the cell could produce: it evacuated the cell in a tenth of a second and left a mound where a sheet should have been, worse the higher the plateau, because the head WAS the cliff. Measured from the sill, that same edge is driven by the half step actually standing above it, and a sheet drains off a four step step and a thirty step cliff at exactly the same rate. That depth factor is what makes the BED shape the flow rather than only steer it — an edge carries in proportion to the water standing above the higher of its two grounds, so a ridge with an inch over it throttles the lake behind it instead of draining it at the lake's full weight, and a wave runs at `sqrt(gravity * depth)`: quickly across a deep pool, crawling over a shoal. The consequence you notice is channelling — cut a gully down a hillside and a sheet released across the top finds it, because the water that reaches it is deeper and deeper water pulls harder. In the editor, two gullies down a hill hold 70% of everything that gets past its foot, in four rows out of sixty-four. **The bed has its own drag**, Chézy-shaped — quadratic in the flow and falling off with the depth, applied implicitly so a quadratic cannot overshoot through zero — so a river barely notices the ground it runs over while a sheet a fraction as deep is stopped by it: fed down the same slope, a 3-deep channel runs at 2.4 tiles a second against a 0.4-deep film's 1.2, and with no bed at all the film is if anything the faster of the two. It is the GROUND's drag, one value for the map; `drag` is the FLUID's, per material, and the two are not interchangeable — a flat per-second retention takes the same fraction off a torrent and a trickle. It is also what stops a flood emptying a basin past the sill that should hold it: momentum alone overshoots, and what crosses a ridge cannot come back. Because the bed now does the dissipating, the FLUID's own damping is weak — it was carrying all of it, and a disturbed basin lost 99% of its motion in twenty seconds, which is water behaving like jelly; at 0.9 a quarter of a disturbance survives a minute and that minute is spent ringing in the basin's own standing waves, whose periods come from its shape for free.
- **A breaking wave LOSES energy, which is the sink the scheme was missing.** Foam (`render/foam.ts`) only ever drew breaking: it reads the flow, paints it white and tells the water nothing. Real breaking is not a colour — it is where a wave's organised motion is turned into turbulence and lost, and in deep water it is the dominant sink there is, the thing that stops a sea growing without bound under its own wind. Without it the only sinks here are a flat per-second drag on the flux and a bed friction that falls off as one over the depth SQUARED, so deep water has almost nothing taking energy out of it at all — and what it does have is blind to scale, taking the same fraction per second off a ten tile swell as off a one column spike. So water poured in stood up in spikes twelve half steps tall and shed them only slowly after you stopped. The model is an eddy viscosity (Kennedy, Chen, Kirby & Dalrymple 2000, after Zelt 1991): a column is breaking when its surface moves faster than a fraction of the speed the wave is travelling at, and while it does, its momentum is diffused into the columns around it at `delta^2 * h * d(eta)/dt` — a mixing length squared over a time, where the length is the depth. It is scale SELECTIVE in the way the drags are not, because a one column spike has an enormous second derivative and a ten tile swell has almost none. **Two thresholds, not one**: breaking starts hard at 0.65 of the wave speed and stops soft at 0.15, sliding between them over five depths travelled, because on a single threshold a wave flickers in and out several times a second and a dissipation that switches at the grid's own rate is exactly the thing a scheme rings on. And **implicit**, by two Jacobi sweeps, which is the whole difference between this working and not. The explicit form has a stability limit of a diffusion number of an eighth and the modelled viscosity runs a couple of hundred times past it, so clamped, every breaking column got the SAME maximum smoothing whether it was barely breaking or coming apart — the model's nought-to-one ramp meant nothing, and scaling the coefficient by a hundred changed the answer by a third, which is the signature of a term that is saturated rather than graded. What it damaged it damaged at every setting: a drain lost a third of its throughput and rivers stopped running. Solved implicitly the answer is BOUNDED however large the viscosity — even one sweep is an average of numbers already present, so it can smooth but never overshoot — and the drain draws its full rate again. Two other things had to be right. It diffuses the VELOCITY and not the discharge, because the published term diffuses `h u` and divides by `h`, and diffusing the discharge alone moves momentum between columns of very different depth as though they were the same water, which over rolling ground is most of the pairs there are. And it exchanges only between WET neighbours: read a dry cell as a velocity of zero and every waterline becomes a wall for the turbulence to drag the flow down against, and a river is nearly all bank. Measured, it is exactly as selective as it should be — a gentle wave is untouched to within 2%, a wind-blown pond settles at the identical chop of 0.164, and a wave big enough to break loses 40% of its crest in the first second. Poured on for ten seconds, the surface roughness goes from twelve half steps to two. It costs 0.5ms a frame on a settled flooded 64² map and 0.75ms while something is actually pouring.
- **Nothing else puts energy IN, so the water is never quite still.** Every other term is a sink, and a solver made only of sinks answers "what does water do when you leave it alone" with "nothing, forever". A wind stress does: a couple of long gusts drifting across the map at periods that do not divide into one another, so the balance the water is chasing keeps moving and it never arrives — a steady wind would tilt a pond once and go quiet again. It pushes the FLUX, so not a drop is created or lost, and the motion is whatever each basin makes of the forcing rather than a look imposed on it. Gated by depth, which is a deliberate lie: a stress on the surface does not care what is under it, but ungated a gust strong enough to stir a lake threw a puddle around in waves as deep as the puddle, and the things that really hold a puddle still — friction and having no fetch to raise a wave over — are not things half-tile cells can represent. Momentum on the edges is what makes a river run rather than ooze. **The step is sized by the deepest water present, because a wave runs at `sqrt(gravity * depth)`** and the deepest water is the fastest thing in the field. Once a wave outruns a cell in a step the scheme rings instead of settling, and it rings in a way that looks exactly like a damping failure and is not one — it survives the wind being turned off, the bed drag raised eightfold and the fluid drag doubled, and it goes away entirely at half the substep. **Lower the ground under a pool and you reach it in four clicks**, because lowering deepens the water by however far you lowered: a pool that settled at 24 half steps deep stood up in spikes thirty half steps tall at 45, and the ringing pumped volume as well as energy, digging its own pit to 112 half steps where the lowering had made 58. So the substep is `Courant * cell / sqrt(gravity * depth)` rather than a flat sixtieth, and the deepest column is tracked by the divergence, which writes every depth anyway. The Courant number is 0.4, which is not the one the theory gives you: the condition is `c dt / dx <= 1` in one dimension and `1/sqrt(2)` on a square grid, and on FLAT ground that second number is about right — a pond at 0.69 settles and one at 0.80 never does. Over rolling ground it is nowhere near, because the bed here is a STAIRCASE (height belongs to a tile and there are four columns to a tile, so every tile boundary is a step under the water) and every step reflects a little of every wave that crosses it. Measured on one scene poured to different depths, 0.46 settles to a millimetre of chop and 0.49 stands up in spikes and stays there; the same relief smoothed to column resolution is stable throughout, which is what says it is the steps and not the slope. Stepping finer costs time rather than truth, and only as the square root of the depth — a pond four times deeper needs twice the substeps, so a flooded 64² map costs 2.2ms at six half steps deep, 5ms at twenty and 6.4ms at a hundred and twenty. `hMax` survives as a BACKSTOP for when a frame runs out of substeps, at the theoretical limit rather than the working one, and computed from the frame's cap and not the substep so that it is UNIFORM: a cap that lands on the water's own depth is worse than one miles above it, because then some edges are capped and their neighbours are not and the switching is itself a thing a scheme can ring on. Measured, a 96 deep pond with the cap at 96 came apart after six seconds while the same pond with the cap at 50 — every edge capped, all of them consistently — sat still. Wet/dry fronts — where these schemes usually break — are handled by scaling each cell's outflows down to the water it actually has *before* anything is applied, so depths are non-negative by construction; one pass suffices, because reducing an outflow can only reduce a neighbour's inflow. A minimum SLOPE stands in for friction, which is what gives a puddle an edge instead of a film that spreads forever; a minimum DEPTH was tried first and makes water cling halfway down a cliff, since a hillside always has a gradient but not always depth.
- **The solver works in TILES, so resolution is a detail setting.** Each column knows how wide it is (`cell`, in tiles) and every length is divided by it: a head is the surface difference across one cell but what accelerates water is the GRADIENT, a flux crosses one edge but the depth it changes is spread over one cell's area, and the friction threshold is a slope rather than a head. Without this, `COLUMNS_PER_TILE` was a physics setting in disguise — halving the cell halved the head across it for the same hillside, so a finer grid was a slower, stickier fluid, and the same pour off the same plateau settled over 986 tiles at two columns and 385 at four. It now settles over the same ground at one, two and four, and gets there at the same rate; what changes is the fineness of the surface and the thinness of the fringe it resolves. Gravity is in tiles per second squared, so a wave runs at `sqrt(gravity)` tiles a second and nothing about the grid can alter that.
- **The mesh is the simulation's output.** Every column holding any water contributes a quad — the sheet FADES out as it thins rather than switching off at a threshold. A hard cutoff is invisible on a shoreline, where the fringe is a thin ring, and ruinous on a sheet that is that depth all over, which is what a raised tile holds: it punched holes in the sheet and you could see the tile through them. That went unnoticed for five commits because it took a SOLVER change to expose it — before hydrostatic reconstruction water evacuated a raised tile rather than sitting on it, so there was no thin sheet to put holes in. 329 columns ringed on all four sides by water they were not drawn with; none now. A quad's corners are the interpolated surface of the columns around it — averaging only WET neighbours, or the shoreline gets dragged down toward the ground. **A CORNER IS A CORNER**: every column meeting at one draws it at the same height, or the mesh comes apart. It carries TWO values, split by the BED its contributors stand on — the average over those on the highest bed, and the average over the rest — because one corner can touch two bodies of water with nothing to do with each other, a sheet on a plateau and a lake at the foot of its cliff, and no single height serves both. The rule lives in `render/corner-rule.ts` and nowhere else — along with the SIDE rule below, which is written in terms of it — because it was written out THREE times — once in the mesh builder, once in WGSL, once in GLSL — and by the third it was already wrong, which is what the hairline below was. There are two copies now and no more: `resolveCorner` is the rule in TypeScript, which the mesh builder calls and the tests pin down, and `cornerRuleSource` is the same rule as shader text, generated once and emitted in whichever dialect asks — so the two shader languages cannot disagree with each other at all, and a test proves it by reducing both to their skeleton and comparing. They sit a screen apart in one file, which is the only arrangement that makes a change to one an obvious omission in the other. **The two heights are ONE again wherever the lower water reaches the higher bed**, which is the same sill test the solver measures heads from: a body is separate when there is AIR between it and the other one's floor, and under a pool deep enough to drown a dip in the bed there is none. Split all the same, the columns either side of the dip get two heights for the same corner, differing only by the surface's own ripple — up to 0.57 half steps, with the LOWER group the higher one 39% of the time — and a corner that does not agree with itself is a crack. The CPU builder hides it under a side face, so it only shows on the vertex-shader path, where nine pixels of grass come through twenty half steps of water as a hairline along every dip. A bed belongs to a whole tile, so neighbouring columns always land in the same group and always draw the corner at the same height. It was a per-COLUMN threshold before — a corner far from a column's own surface was dropped in favour of that surface — and a threshold is a decision each column makes for itself: put a wave through a pond and the crest crosses it while the trough beside it does not, so the two stop sharing the corner between them and the sheet splits open. Nothing at rest, 2.3% of edges torn at a modest wave, 19% at a big one, gaps up to two hundred pixels of ground showing through the water. A corner is also never below the BED the column stands on, which is the one thing that cannot happen: over a one step rise the averaged corner is near enough to pass any threshold and still under the tile's own top face, so the sheet's edge sank into it. The sides of a body of water hang from the very corners the surface quad used, so the two share vertices and there is no seam, and each end of a side reaches DOWN TO THE CORNER THE NEIGHBOUR ITSELF DRAWS, never below this column's own bed. That rule sits beside the corner rule and is generated the same way, in one TypeScript function and one shader template rather than three transcriptions; the tops come back already clamped to their own floors, so a side that should not be drawn at all is one whose two ends are both zero height — which the shader can draw regardless, since a zero-height quad makes no fragments, and which saves the mesh builder a quad it would only have to blank. The rule was "skip the side wherever the water next door reaches this column's bed, because then the two are one body and the surface covers the join" — a guess, and one that contradicts the corner split, which deliberately gives two beds two different heights for the same corner. Water running down a staircase tore along every step's seam: rasterised, 2.7% of the ground under interior water showed through, and every sample of it was on a tile with a one step drop, hard against that edge. Matched corner for corner to what the neighbour actually draws there is nothing left to leave showing — 0.1%, four samples of four and a half thousand, all of them exactly on a polygon boundary. Getting that wrong either way is expensive: a step's worth of height before drawing anything — left a shallow sheet on a raised tile hanging over it with nothing joining the two, a panel of water floating above the ground it was lying on; any height at all drew an edge wherever two neighbours differed by a hair, which on moving water is everywhere — sixty-eight thousand of them on a flat flooded map against sixty-five thousand pieces of surface. So the sheet rises, falls, tilts and retreats as the water does. **A current CARRIES a pattern** (`render/flow-wash.ts`), which is what makes it read as flowing — the solver knew the velocity of every column and the sheet lying on them ignored it, so a standing flow read as a still ribbon. The obvious way to draw one is a plane wave: bands across the flow whose phase is the corner's position projected onto the flow direction. It does not work and cannot be made to — the phase is `position · direction`, so two neighbouring columns forty tiles from the origin whose flow differs by eight degrees are four whole cycles apart. Measured, one column's shade correlated with its neighbour's at **0.002**: television static, which is exactly what it looked like. So the pattern is not computed from where a column is, it is carried. A still field of smooth mottling is what water looks like when nothing is happening to it; every frame the live field is traced back up the current, sampled, and pulled a little towards the still one. Moving water smears it into streaks along its own path and still water settles back. It cannot go noisy, because every value is an interpolation between values that were already smooth: the correlation is 0.79. Nothing in the still field is finer than a tile, either — at four columns to the tile a pattern the mesh cannot resolve is noise however coherent it started. **And the waves reveal it.** Shaded in proportion to their slope, a pond's ripples came out at four percent of the range and could not be seen, so what you got was a mottle sitting still while the mesh rippled underneath it — a texture painted on the world rather than on the water. The lean of the surface is read along BOTH axes now, since up-screen in this projection is up and left together, and through a response that is steep near flat and saturates, because the coefficient that makes a pond's ripples visible sends a river's standing waves off the end of the scale. The carried pattern is then shown where the water is moving AND where it is leaning, so a wave passing over still water brings the mottle out on its own face and lets it go behind — what you see travels with the wave although what it is made of stays where it is, which is how water works. A rippled pond went from a shade range of nine steps in thirty-one with one percent of its corners changing from one third of a second to the next, to twenty-six steps with forty-seven percent. Shades are looked up rather than computed: lightening a colour and packing it is thirty-odd operations and there are a quarter of a million corners a frame on a flooded map, so there is a table of shades of each fluid — thirty-two for the water itself and twenty-four more above them, for foam. The vertical edges are TWO things, and conflating them was a long-lived bug. The SIDE of a body of water runs from its surface to the ground it is standing on and no further — what is below that is rock, and running it down to whatever the NEIGHBOUR stood on painted a pond on a plateau over the whole cliff beneath it, worse the higher the ground was, because the excess was exactly the height of the drop. The FALL below a lip is drawn off the FLUX and not off the height difference: a height difference is a cliff, and a waterfall is water going over one. Both are full width at the top — the whole edge of the column is what the water leaves by, and inset from both sides they were a row of ribbons with the rock showing between them — and a fall narrows and fades below by how hard it is pouring and how far it has to go, since past a few half steps a fall is mostly air. A fall is drawn whether or not the column is deep enough to show a surface, because water accelerates and thins as it goes over a lip and at the lip itself it usually is not. And a fall TAKES TIME to come down (`fluid/falls.ts`) — the WATER as well as the mesh. Water went over a lip and arrived at the bottom in the same frame, so a cliff was either bare or curtained with no moment in between: the wave at the top stopped and the wave at the bottom started, and nothing crossed the distance. A fall has a HEAD and a FRONT, both measured down from the lip and both accelerating under a gravity of its own — the solver's is a pressure gradient in tiles and has nothing to say about anything falling. While water is going over, the head stays at the lip and the front runs away from it, so a tongue of water reaches down the wall at the speed a thing falls: a twelve half-step drop takes 0.53s against a predicted 0.52s. When the flow stops the head lets go and falls too, so what is left detaches and drops away rather than being sucked back up; it clings for a sixth of a second first, because the flux over an edge flickers across zero and without that the fall stutters several times a second. The sheet thins with the distance fallen, so a long one is bright at the lip and gone before the floor. The water is IN THE AIR while it falls: it leaves the column at the top and is not in the one below until the front has gone the distance, and `totalWater` counts it, because a drop in the air is still a drop. Off a twenty half-step cliff, nothing at all arrives below for the first 0.67 seconds — the time that drop takes — and the total reads 48.000 at every sample throughout. A fall is two terrain steps or more; one is a ledge water tumbles over and keeps flowing, and at a tenth of that threshold a steady hillside became a staircase of little cliffs with the water spending its whole journey in the air. A column with water in the air off one of its edges stays in the active box even when nothing is standing on it — dropped out, the fall stops being stepped and whatever is falling hangs there for ever, which is what happened to a waterfall the moment its shelf ran dry. Faces are FULL WIDTH top and bottom, so a run of them along an edge meets and reads as one sheet; tapered into the drop they were a row of wedges side by side, which looks like bunting. How much water is involved is in the OPACITY, and in how it shades from the top of a face to its foot. Both are only lightly aerated: whitened as hard as spray, the side of a still pool came out at RGB(171,199,215) against water's own (42,111,151), and a ring of near-white round every pool on a plateau is what made them read as panels stuck to the rock rather than water standing on it. Everything that used to be derived and drawn as decoration — pools, levels, waterfalls, chutes — is gone, because all of it was a consequence of solving waves on a flat plane per body of water, and a plane is a thing water cannot flow off.
- **Water goes WHITE where it breaks, and the white is a thing in its own right** (`render/foam.ts`). Foam is not a shade of water, it is something that happens to water and then sits on it: a crest breaks, and what it leaves drifts off downstream and fades long after the wave that made it has gone. Computed per frame from the surface — whiten whatever is steep right now — it reads as a highlight painted on the mesh, appearing and vanishing with the geometry underneath. So it is a FIELD, born in one place, carried by the current on the same backward trace the flow wash uses, and decaying on its own clock. **Where it is born is the SOLVER's answer, not this file's.** For a while it was both: this file carried three criteria of its own — a crest too tall for the water under it (the depth-limited index `H/h ≈ 0.78`, McCowan 1894), a front outrunning its own waves (the Froude number, which is the whole of open channel hydraulics), and a wave too steep to hold its shape (Stokes' limiting `H/L` of a seventh) — while the solver carried a fourth for the dissipation it does when a wave breaks. Two tests of the same thing in two places, and they did not agree: on a pour they fired on 46% and 58% of the water with 30% of it in common, and on a waterfall on 8% and 4% with 2% in common. So there was white where nothing was being dissipated, and dissipation with nothing to show for it. Breaking belongs to the solver because there it has consequences, and this file reads `broke`, the nought-to-one intensity the solver already works out — so the water goes white exactly where it is losing energy. It draws slightly MORE foam than the three criteria did, which was the surprise, and the worry beforehand had been the opposite: the solver's test needs the surface to be MOVING, so a steady standing wave should have gone dark. It does not, because nothing here is steady at column scale — a steady river comes out 35% white against 28%, a waterfall 44% against 31%, a big wave 22% against 17%, and a settled pond nought either way. It also took 329 lines out of this file and 1.3ms off the draw, because the tile-summary pass the steepness test needed went with it. What the retired criteria taught is worth keeping even though the code has gone. A crest test alone is blind on flat ground, and flat ground is where you pour — nothing on a flat sheet stands above the sheet. A Froude test alone is blind in still deep water. A steepness test asked at the GRID scale measures the scheme's own odd-even ripple and nothing else: a row through a disturbed basin read `8.34 7.36 8.18 7.44 8.07`, a zigzag of half a step per column, a slope of 0.49, whiter than Stokes, and the whole pond went white and stayed white. And any of them compared against neighbours has to be taught that a cliff is not a trough, or every raised puddle grows a white rim. **The one thing still decided here is the foot of a waterfall**, which is not a wave breaking at all — water arriving out of the air has air in it, and the solver has no opinion because landing water is put into the column directly rather than through the divergence its test reads. It needs a scale, though. Flat out, a walled basin grew a permanent white fringe: a film a thirtieth of a half step deep had crept onto the top of the wall and was trickling back in over a ten step drop, which IS a fall and does land, but lands a trickle. The edges of a fed waterfall carry two to five units in the air and that fringe carried 0.003 to 0.04, so landing foam is scaled by what is arriving. Foam is MIXED towards the white end of the ramp rather than added on top of the water's own shade. Added on, it had to start from wherever the lighting had got to — halfway up a ramp of thirty-two — so a fully broken crest landed at 40 of 55 and came out RGB(174) against water's own ceiling of 138: barely paler than water, for the whitest thing the renderer can draw. That was the whole of "I poured on a flat plane and there is no foam" — the foam was there, at 0.7 of full, and it was being drawn four shades above ordinary water. Mixed, foam of one is the top of the ramp whatever was underneath it. Foam whitens AND closes the surface: white water is full of air and air is not something you see through, and a crest that went pale but stayed as transparent as the pond behind it read as a highlight rather than as foam. It can only close what the water is already covering, gated by the same fade that brings a thin sheet in. A fed waterfall's plunge pool draws corners at RGB(210,225,233) against water's own (42,111,151), at 224 alpha where the water beside it is at 118.
- **The surface is a real mesh, not `Graphics`** (`render/quads.ts`). A quad batch per band writes vertices straight into one interleaved buffer — position plus a packed colour — and uploads only the bytes it filled, drawing them with a small flat-shaded shader. `Graphics` was tried first and its cost is not the triangles, it is REBUILDING: every frame it walks an instruction list, tessellates each polygon, recomputes bounds and uploads a whole new geometry. Measured on a flooded 64² map that was 100ms a frame against 1.5ms for the solver behind it, and it barely moved between 648 quads and 2564 — the signature of per-rebuild overhead rather than per-triangle work. The mesh does the same map in 2.2ms. Capacity grows by doubling and never shrinks, so a settled surface allocates nothing; quads dropped since the last frame are collapsed to a point, because the index buffer spans the whole capacity and anything left behind would keep drawing. `visible` is only touched when a band goes from having water to having none, since visibility is structural and flipping it rebuilds the scene's instruction list.
- **The mesh is built in a VERTEX SHADER** (`render/water-gpu.ts`). The CPU builder in `render/water.ts` is still here and still the reference the render tests hold to account — it is not dead code, it is the answer this path is checked against, and `?cpuwater=1` is how to get back to it. It began as a measurement of what moving the work would be worth. Of `drawWater`'s 9.15ms on a flooded 64² map, 3.29ms is working the corners out and 3.71ms is writing the vertices — 7ms of arithmetic whose whole life is to become 3.1MB of bytes the GPU reads once and the CPU throws away. The prototype computes them where they are used. The draw is PROCEDURAL: there is no per-frame vertex data at all, every band draws a fixed run of vertices, and each one works out from its own index which tile of the band it belongs to, which column of that tile and which corner of that column, then reads the simulation out of textures and computes its own position and colour. Per band because the scene is sorted by diagonal and one draw for the whole map would put every drop either in front of everything or behind it; a band is `x + y` in tiles, so its tiles are `(tx, b - tx)` over a range and that range is all a vertex needs to find itself. **Nothing is copied to upload it** — each texture is a view straight onto an array the solver already owns, which is why the state goes up as six single-channel textures rather than one packed four-channel one: packing would mean interleaving half a million floats a frame, which is the work this is meant to remove. **Which bands draw at all is the one thing this path has to be told**, and the CPU builder works it out for itself by walking the wet columns. Left out, every band drew its whole complement of vertices every frame however little water there was and however little of the map was on screen — and a map is mostly dry most of the time, which is the case that matters. Two ranges, intersected: the camera's, from the band renderer that was built around that question, and the water's, from the solver's active box — a band is `x + y` in tiles, so the box's corners bound which bands can hold anything. Measured across the range, both paths in one session on a 64² map: an empty map costs neither of them anything (0.00ms either way), a small pond costs the shader about 0.4ms MORE — the price of a procedural draw, which walks what could exist rather than what does — and a flooded map costs it **3.8ms against 26.9ms**, taking the whole water frame from 34.5ms to 11.3ms. That is with the sides and the falls drawn, which the first measurement of this was done without. What is left of the 5.25 is almost exactly the two carried fields, which are still the CPU's — the mesh build itself is near enough free. The browser figures are about 2.2× the headless ones throughout, because a preview that is not on screen is deprioritised; the ratio is the part to trust. It draws the whole surface now — the sheet, the SIDES of it and the FALLS over its lips — so the two paths put the same geometry on the screen. A column gets five quads rather than one: a procedural draw cannot skip, so a part that is not there collapses its four vertices onto a point and costs four vertex shader invocations and no fragments. That is the trade this path makes everywhere: the CPU builder walks what exists, this walks what could exist, and a nearly empty map costs it what a flooded one does. Everything both paths must agree about is imported from `water.ts` rather than copied, because two copies of the corner rule is exactly what put a hairline of grass along every dip in the bed on one path and not the other. Pixi will not generate a usable bind group layout for this on its own, either — it hardcodes every texture to `visibility: FRAGMENT` and `sampleType: "float"`, and these are read in the VERTEX stage from `r32float`, which WebGPU classes as unfilterable. Getting that wrong invalidates the pipeline, which invalidates the command buffer, which draws not merely no water but no world at all. **There is a WebGL2 version of the whole thing**, because a browser without WebGPU has to get water and not a blank map, and it is a transliteration on purpose: the same functions in the same order doing the same arithmetic, so a change to one is an obvious change to the other. `textureLoad` becomes `texelFetch`, `select(f, t, c)` becomes `c ? t : f`, and the uniform group arrives as plain uniforms rather than a struct. Two things had to be right and neither announces itself. The float textures must be NEAREST: `texelFetch` never filters, but WebGL2 will not call an `R32F` texture complete if it is asked to filter one without `OES_texture_float_linear`, and an incomplete texture reads as black. And the globals must be PLAIN UNIFORMS and not a `uniform globalUniforms { ... }` block — Pixi hands them to a WebGL program one at a time, its own shaders are built that way, and declared as a block nothing binds it: the driver says "used but unbound uniform buffer" and drops the draw. That second one had been true of the CPU mesh in `quads.ts` since the day it was written, so BOTH water paths drew nothing at all on WebGL and neither did anywhere else, because the editor asks for WebGPU and gets it. `?webgl=1` makes the other renderer one URL away, which is the only reason this was ever going to be found.
- **The two paths are checked against each other in PIXELS** (`debug/water-compare.ts`, `window.__waterCompare()` in dev). The shader is the path that ships and the mesh builder is the one the render tests hold to account, which is the wrong way round unless something ties them together. The shared rules tie the DECISIONS together; this ties the pictures together. One scene with a dip in the bed under a deep pool, a plateau with a sheet on it beside a lake, and a lip for that sheet to go over — drawn twice into two render textures with nothing else in the frame, read back through the renderer's own extract, compared pixel for pixel. It is the only check that covers the arithmetic which can only live in a shader: the projection, the shading, the geometry of a fall. The two are allowed to differ, because the builder computes in doubles and the shader in floats and a corner half a millimetre apart in world space lands a fraction of a pixel apart on screen — so the measure is how MANY pixels differ and by how much. **Right, it reads: of 33,115 pixels either path draws, the worst single channel anywhere disagrees by 3 out of 255 and the mean by 0.4, nothing differs by more than 4, and neither path draws a pixel the other leaves empty — the same on both renderers, to the pixel.** Wrong, it reads 1,652 pixels differing, 5% of what was drawn, the worst by 248, with pixels on each side the other never drew: that is the corner merge put back the way it shipped, present in the builder and missing from the shader, because a comparison that cannot fail is worth nothing. It cannot run under `bun test`, which has no GPU at all, so it is a harness rather than a test — what a browser test runner would add is the running of it, not the substance.
- **Water is live state, not a map layer.** Depth is a float that changes every frame, so it sits outside the grid the way the road network does: pouring is undoable, the flowing is not. The `fluid` layer records what was poured where, for saving a map; the depth is the simulation's.
- **Springs and drains are a RATE, and they are a layer.** A pour is a volume that arrives once, so what it makes runs out; a spring keeps arriving, so what it makes is a standing flow — a river is a source up high and a sink at the bottom, not a mode. One signed number per cell, positive in and negative out, because a drain is the same mechanism backwards; it is in half steps a second over the TILE, and resolution-independent because the same depth goes on every one of the tile's columns. Being an ordinary layer is what makes a spring undoable and saveable while the water it produces is neither, so a river reloads as a river rather than as the puddle it happened to be when you saved. A drain takes its rate only while water stands on it — it is a plughole, not a vacuum.
- **The edge of the map is the edge of the world, not a wall.** The outermost ring of columns is emptied before every substep — an absorbing boundary, a depth held at zero — so water that reaches it has left. Without it a spring fills the world and the only way out is a hole you dug, which is fine for a reservoir and useless for a river. On by default and toggleable; the solver itself defaults to walled, because a test of how much water there is should not depend on where the water happened to be. It lets water off, it does not suck: on a flat plain friction still leaves a film, and only ground that slopes towards the edge drains dry. With it, a spring at the head of a valley and an open edge at its foot reach a standing flow — 262 units on the map at thirty seconds and 263 at ninety, with sixteen a second going in and the same going off the side. That is a river, and it is a source, a slope and a boundary rather than a feature.
- **Build cursor.** All placement routes through `world/edit/cursor.ts`: the tool supplies cells, the cursor draws them. The footprint outline lives in the overlay *above* the world so it stays readable, while ghost sprites are parented into their own cells' bands so they are occluded at true depth. Cells are tinted **individually**, so a partly blocked footprint shows which cells are the problem. It redraws on change (a signature over cells, heights, validity, frame and scale), not per frame.

## Dev tools

- A **building-kit labeller** (dev-only, `dev/roadlabel.html`) authors `building-kits.json` / `road-labels.json` / `slope-labels.json` for the renderers; its Slopes tab labels form, rise (×0.5 / ×1 only), direction, per-edge state and tunnels. ⚠️ These files hold hand-authored data — never overwrite them programmatically (e.g. via preview eval).
- The **world editor** at `?world=1` is the authoring surface for World v2: paint any of ~930 atlas frames, raise/lower/flatten/smooth terrain, place and demolish structures, brush shapes and sizes (a **road** line drag bends once into an L, since cells touching only at a corner are not adjacent here and would lay lone squares rather than a road), undo/redo, save/load, resize, pave and unpave roads (ramps appear on their own where a road crosses a step), overlays for the grid, bands, height, iso origin, road mask and network components, a per-cell inspector that reports which autotile rule fired, and **fixtures** that build a whole map on demand. The terrain ones (ziggurat / occluder / ramp fan / road shapes / avenue / plaza / split trap) make a picking or connectivity bug reproducible; the WATER ones (river / cascade / lake / islands) arrive with their own taps in the `source` layer, so loading one starts a flow rather than handing you a shape to pour into — a graded valley reaching a standing flow, terraces each holding a pool at its lip, a basin fed from a shelf and drained through a notch, and blocks at one, two, four and eight steps each pouring off its own edges into water that is already there, which is the bed for checking what water on a raised tile looks like. In dev it also exposes the store as `window.__world` — a console `await import()` of the store resolves through a *different* Vite module graph and returns a second, unrelated instance, so that handle is the only reliable way to inspect the live map from outside React.
- **`bun run bake:road-corners`** regenerates `isometric_assets/derived/roadCorners_sheet.{png,xml}` from the landscape sheet. Derived output, checked in; re-run it if the road labelling or the notch rects change. `dev/png-min.ts` is a ~120-line PNG codec used only by the bakers — the project ships no image library and this avoids adding one for a build script.
- Some dev affordances (e.g. "grant juice") are gated to `localhost` via `utils/dev-mode.ts`.
